Many ideas about Canadian government are close to true, or borrowed from other
countries, especially the United States. Each myth below has a short correction and a
link to the page that explains it in full.

## Government & the Crown

### "Canada isn't tied to the monarchy anymore."
**Actually:** It still is. The King is King of Canada, a title legally separate from
his British one. His role is almost entirely ceremonial, but the whole legal system is
built around the Crown. [Read more](/learn/crown)

### "The Governor General is the head of state."
**Actually:** The King is [head of state](term:head-of-state). The Governor General represents him and
exercises almost all his powers in Canada, which is why the line can seem blurry.
[Read more](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### "Lieutenant Governors work for the Governor General."
**Actually:** They're appointed by the Governor General on the Prime Minister's advice,
but each one represents the King directly in their own province and acts on the advice
of the provincial government. They don't report to the Governor General.
[Read more](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### "The monarchy could be dropped by a simple vote."
**Actually:** The Crown is woven through the Constitution. Changing "the office of the
Queen" needs the agreement of Parliament and all ten provincial legislatures
(Constitution Act, 1982, s. 41(a)). [Read more](/learn/crown/republic-question)

### "Canada's Constitution is the Charter."
**Actually:** The Charter is one part of the Constitution Act, 1982. The Constitution
also includes the Constitution Act, 1867, other written texts, and the unwritten
conventions that shape how government works. [Read more](/learn/constitution)

### "The Constitution spells out how the government works."
**Actually:** Only partly. The Prime Minister, cabinet, and the rule that a government
needs the House's confidence mostly come from unwritten [conventions](term:constitutional-convention), not written text.
Breaking them would cause a crisis even though no court would enforce them.
[Read more](/learn/constitution/conventions)

## Parliament & elections

### "Canadians vote for the Prime Minister."
**Actually:** You vote only for your local MP. The Prime Minister is whoever can hold
the confidence of the House of Commons, which is normally the leader of the party with
the most seats. [Read more](/learn/elections)

### "The party with the most votes wins."
**Actually:** Seats, not votes, decide who governs. In each riding the candidate with
the most votes wins, even without a majority, so a party can win the most votes
nationally and still win fewer seats. This happened federally in 2019 and 2021.
[Read more](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### "If the governing party changes leaders, there must be an election."
**Actually:** No. The new party leader becomes Prime Minister and governs as long as
they keep the House's confidence. [Read more](/learn/elections/party-leaders)

### "The government and Parliament are the same thing."
**Actually:** The government (the Prime Minister and cabinet) sits *in* Parliament but
isn't the same as it. Parliament is the King, the Senate and the House of Commons, and
it includes opposition MPs and senators who can vote the government out.
[Read more](/learn/parliament)

### "The Senate is just a rubber stamp."
**Actually:** Senators regularly amend bills, and the House often accepts those
changes. Outright defeat of a bill passed by the House is rare, but it has happened.
[Read more](/learn/parliament/senate)

### "A minority government is illegitimate, or temporary by nature."
**Actually:** A minority government has exactly the same legal powers as a majority. It
just needs support from other parties to pass bills and survive confidence votes.
[Read more](/learn/parliament/confidence)

## Federalism & provinces

### "The provinces work for the federal government."
**Actually:** Each order of government gets its powers directly from the Constitution.
Within their own areas, provinces are fully in charge, and Ottawa can't overrule them
by ordinary law. [Read more](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### "Health care is run by the federal government."
**Actually:** Provinces and territories run health care. Ottawa contributes money
through the [Canada Health Transfer](term:canada-health-transfer) and attaches conditions through the Canada Health
Act, but it doesn't run hospitals or pay doctors.
[Read more](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization)

### "Richer provinces send equalization payments to poorer ones."
**Actually:** Equalization is paid by the federal government from its general
revenues, mostly federal taxes paid by people and businesses everywhere in Canada. No
provincial government pays into the program.
[Read more](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization)

### "Territories are just small provinces."
**Actually:** Provinces' powers are protected by the Constitution. Territories' powers
are delegated by federal laws, and turning a territory into a province would take a
constitutional amendment. [Read more](/learn/federalism/territories)

### "Cities have their own constitutional powers."
**Actually:** They don't. Municipalities are created by provincial laws and can be
reshaped by them, as the Supreme Court confirmed in a 2021 case about Toronto's city
council. [Read more](/learn/federalism/municipalities)

### "Every Canadian legislature runs on political parties."
**Actually:** The Northwest Territories and Nunavut use **[consensus government](term:consensus-government)**.
Candidates run as independents, and after the election the members choose the Premier
and cabinet themselves by secret ballot. [Read more](/provinces/northwest-territories)

## Money & government

### "The Royal Canadian Mint controls Canada's money."
**Actually:** The Mint only makes coins. The **Bank of Canada** is the central bank: it
sets interest rates, steers inflation and issues banknotes.
[Read more](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)

### "The budget is the law that lets the government spend money."
**Actually:** The budget is a plan and a political statement. Legal authority to spend
comes from Parliament passing appropriation acts based on the estimates, and from other
laws that authorize specific spending. [Read more](/learn/government/the-budget)

### "Public servants work for the party in power."
**Actually:** They serve the government of the day, but they're hired on merit and
expected to be non-partisan. When a new party takes over, the public service stays and
serves it just as loyally. [Read more](/learn/government/departments)

### "The Auditor General works for the government."
**Actually:** The Auditor General is independent and reports to Parliament.
Appointment needs consultation with every recognized party and approval by both the
House and the Senate. [Read more](/learn/government/watchdogs)

## Law & courts

### "Canada has separate federal and state courts, like the US."
**Actually:** Canada has one mostly integrated system. Provincial courts apply federal
laws, including the Criminal Code, every day, and appeals from every province can reach
the Supreme Court of Canada. The federal courts are a small, specialized branch.
[Read more](/learn/law/courts)

### "Each province has its own criminal code."
**Actually:** Criminal law is federal, so the Criminal Code is the same across Canada.
Provinces can create **[provincial offences](term:provincial-offence)**, such as traffic and liquor rules, but
these aren't crimes under the Criminal Code. [Read more](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### "Quebec has a completely different legal system."
**Actually:** Only for private law. Quebec uses the [Civil Code](term:civil-code-of-quebec) for things like
contracts, property and family law. Criminal law and constitutional law in Quebec are
the same as everywhere else. [Read more](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### "Judges in Canada are elected."
**Actually:** No Canadian judges are elected. Federal or provincial governments appoint
them, and they hold office until retirement so they can rule without fear of losing
their jobs. [Read more](/learn/law/judges)

### "Only the Supreme Court can strike down a law."
**Actually:** Other courts can also rule that a law is unconstitutional, though usually
only for the case in front of them. Supreme Court rulings matter most because they bind
every other court in the country. [Read more](/learn/law)

## Rights

### "The Charter protects me from my employer, my landlord or a store."
**Actually:** The Charter applies only to governments and bodies acting as government.
Private discrimination is covered by federal and provincial human rights laws instead.
[Read more](/learn/charter)

### "Charter rights are absolute."
**Actually:** [Section 1](doc:charter/s1) allows reasonable limits that a government can justify in
court, and section 33 lets legislatures override some rights for five years at a time.
[Read more](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits)

### "The notwithstanding clause can override any right."
**Actually:** It covers only sections 2 and 7 to 15. It can't touch democratic rights,
mobility rights or language rights, including minority language education rights.
[Read more](/learn/charter/notwithstanding-clause)

### "Only Canadian citizens have Charter rights."
**Actually:** Most Charter rights belong to "everyone" in Canada, including
non-citizens. Only a few, such as voting, the right to enter Canada and minority
language education rights, are limited to citizens.
[Read more](/learn/charter/your-rights)

## Indigenous peoples

### "Aboriginal rights were created by the Constitution in 1982."
**Actually:** Section 35 *recognizes and affirms* rights that already existed, many of
them rooted in Indigenous peoples' presence on the land before Europeans arrived. It
didn't invent them. [Read more](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### "Treaties are historical documents with no force today."
**Actually:** Treaty rights are protected by section 35 of the Constitution.
Governments are legally bound by them, and courts enforce them.
[Read more](/learn/indigenous/treaties)

### "Indigenous people don't pay taxes."
**Actually:** Most do. The Indian Act exempts only certain property of *status* First
Nations people located *on a reserve*, such as income connected to a reserve. Métis and Inuit
have no such exemption under the Indian Act.
[Read more](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### "The duty to consult gives Indigenous nations a veto over any project."
**Actually:** The Crown must consult in good faith and, where appropriate, accommodate
concerns, but in most cases the law doesn't require consent. Proven Aboriginal title is
the main exception: there, the Crown needs consent or must meet a strict justification
test. [Read more](/learn/indigenous/section-35)
