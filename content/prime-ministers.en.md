The **Prime Minister** is Canada's head of government: the person who leads the Cabinet
and sets the government's direction. Canadians don't vote for the Prime Minister
directly. The Governor General appoints whoever can hold the **confidence** of the House
of Commons, which in practice means the leader of the party that can win its votes. See
[Cabinet and the Prime Minister's Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo) and
[Confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence) for how that works.

Prime Ministers are numbered by person, not by term. Someone who leaves office and later
returns keeps their original number, which is why there have been more terms than
Prime Ministers.

For the elections that put them there, including which produced majority or minority
governments, see [150 years of federal elections](/elections-history).

### How parties are coloured

Canada's party system has always had a Liberal side and a Conservative side, but the
Conservative side has changed its name several times. The timeline colours each term by
**party family** so the long-run pattern is easy to see:

| Party in office | Years | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Liberal | 1867 to today | Liberal |
| Liberal-Conservative | Macdonald's coalition in the first years after Confederation | Conservative family |
| Conservative | The original Conservative Party, 1867–1942 | Conservative family |
| Unionist | Robert Borden's wartime coalition of Conservatives and Liberals who backed conscription, 1917–1921 | Conservative family |
| Progressive Conservative | 1942–2003 | Conservative family |
| Conservative | Today's party, formed in 2003 when the Progressive Conservatives and the Canadian Alliance merged | Conservative family |

The Unionist government is grouped with the Conservatives because it was led by
Conservative Prime Ministers and most of its members were Conservatives. The table below
lists each party by its own name.

## Why some served more than once

A Prime Minister can lose power and win it back. Three cases explain most of the
returns:

- **John A. Macdonald** resigned in 1873 over the Pacific Scandal, a controversy about
  election money linked to the contract for the transcontinental railway. His party won
  the 1878 election and he governed until his death in 1891.
- **The King–Byng affair (1926).** William Lyon Mackenzie King asked Governor General
  Lord Byng to dissolve Parliament; Byng refused, and King resigned. Arthur Meighen
  formed a government but lost a confidence vote within days, and King won the election
  that followed. It's the best-known use of the Governor General's
  [reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers). See also the
  [glossary](/glossary#king-byng-affair).
- **A minority defeated (1979–80).** Joe Clark's minority government lost a confidence
  vote on its budget in December 1979, and Pierre Trudeau's Liberals won the election
  of February 1980.
