Who lives in Canada, and where, shapes almost everything else on this site. Seats in the
House of Commons are shared out by population, federal transfers follow population, and
an ageing country spends more on health care and pensions. This page shows the long-run
trends using Statistics Canada's own figures.

Two kinds of numbers appear below. A **census** counts everyone on one day: every
ten years from 1871, and every five years since 1956. **Population estimates** start from the census
and add births and arrivals and subtract deaths and departures, to give a figure for
July 1 each year. Estimates also correct for people the census missed, so they run a
little higher.

## Cities and the countryside

At Confederation, most Canadians lived on farms and in villages. The census has tracked
the **urban** population (people in towns and cities) since 1871. Today Statistics
Canada calls these places **population centres**: any place with at least 1,000 people
living at least 400 to the square kilometre.

## Each province's share of Canada

Because seats in the House of Commons are based on population, a province's share of
Canada's population roughly tracks its share of MPs. The Constitution softens the effect
for slower-growing provinces: under the [senatorial clause](/glossary#senatorial-clause),
no province can have fewer MPs than senators, and a "grandfather clause" protects some
provinces from losing seats. See [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island)
for the clearest example, and [Parliament](/learn/parliament) for how the House works.

## Immigration

Canada has always been shaped by immigration, but the pace has changed a lot. Arrivals
surged during the settlement of the West before the First World War, almost stopped
during the Depression and the Second World War, and rose again after 1945. The figures
here count new **permanent residents**; they don't include temporary residents.

<tn-widget name="demographics" data-chart="immigration"></tn-widget>

Where immigrants come from has changed as much as how many arrive. Until the 1960s,
Canada's immigration rules favoured people from Britain, Europe and the United States.
Rules adopted in 1967 introduced a points system that judged applicants on education,
work skills and language instead of their country of origin.

## English, French and other languages

Canada has two official languages, English and French (see
[sections 16 to 22 of the Charter](/learn/charter/your-rights)). The census asks for each
person's **mother tongue**: the first language they learned at home as a child and
still understand. Immigration has made the country more multilingual, so both
official languages now make up a smaller share than they once did.

## First Nations, Métis and Inuit

The Constitution recognizes three **Indigenous peoples**: First Nations, Métis and
Inuit (s. 35(2) of the Constitution Act, 1982). The census asks people whether they
identify as one of them. See [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous) for
what that recognition means.

## An ageing population

Canadians are living longer and having fewer children, so the population is getting
older. The **median age** is the age that splits the population in half: half are
younger, half are older.

A **population pyramid** shows how many people there are at each age. A young,
growing country has a wide base; an older one bulges in the middle and the top.

How old a province's population is matters for its budget. Older populations need more
health care, which provinces run, and pay less income tax. That's part of the debate
over federal health transfers. See [Money and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization).
