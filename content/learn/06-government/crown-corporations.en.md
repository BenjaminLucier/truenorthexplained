## In short

A **Crown corporation** is a company wholly owned by the government. It's used when a
public job is best run like a business, or needs some distance from day-to-day
politics. Each one has its own board and a mandate set by law. The government sets the
big picture and approves its plans, and a minister answers for it in Parliament, but it
manages its own operations. Provinces have Crown corporations too.

## The details

### Why have them?

Governments use the Crown corporation form when an activity:

- sells goods or services, like mail delivery, passenger rail, or mortgage insurance;
- should be run with sound commercial practices, sometimes earning its own revenue;
- needs **independence** from ministers, like public broadcasting;
- serves a public policy goal that private companies wouldn't meet on their own, like
  financing small businesses or exporters.

The core challenge, as the Treasury Board Secretariat puts it, is balancing **public
policy goals** with **commercial efficiency**.

### Some federal examples

| Crown corporation | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Canada Post** | Mail and parcel delivery |
| **CBC/Radio-Canada** | Public broadcaster in English and French |
| **VIA Rail Canada** | Intercity passenger trains |
| **Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC)** | Mortgage insurance and housing programs |
| **Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC)** | Financing and advice for small and medium businesses |
| **Export Development Canada (EDC)** | Financing and insurance for exporters |
| **[Royal Canadian Mint](org:royal-canadian-mint)** | Coins (see [the Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)) |
| **[Bank of Canada](org:bank-of-canada)** | The central bank; a special case with its own law |

### How they're governed

Most federal Crown corporations follow the rules in **Part X of the *Financial
Administration Act***, plus their own founding law (usually a special [Act of
Parliament](term:act-of-parliament)):

- **Board of directors.** Oversees the corporation and leaves day-to-day decisions to
  its executives.
- **Corporate plan.** Each year the board approves a plan, the responsible minister
  recommends it, and the federal [cabinet](org:cabinet) must approve it before it takes effect.
- **A responsible minister.** The link between the corporation, cabinet and Parliament.
  The minister tables the corporation's annual report and answers questions about it in
  Parliament.
- **Directives.** In rare cases, cabinet can formally **direct** a parent Crown
  corporation to do something in the public interest (s. 89 of the Act). The minister
  must consult the board first, and the directive must be tabled in Parliament, so it's
  public.
- **Audits.** Crown corporations are audited, often by the [Auditor General](org:auditor-general).

Some corporations get extra protection. The *Broadcasting Act*, for example, protects
CBC/Radio-Canada's journalistic, creative and programming independence. The Bank of
Canada is mainly governed by its own Act rather than by Part X.

Most Crown corporations are **agents of the Crown**, which means they act on the
government's behalf legally and generally share its privileges, such as not paying
provincial or municipal taxes.

### Provincial Crown corporations

Provinces use the same tool, often for big utilities and services:

- **Electricity:** Hydro-Québec, BC Hydro, and others.
- **Alcohol and cannabis sales:** provincial liquor boards such as the LCBO in Ontario

### Selling them off

Governments can also **privatize** Crown corporations, selling them to private
investors. Federal examples include Air Canada (late 1980s), Petro-Canada (starting in
1991) and Canadian National Railway (1995). <!-- check: privatization dates; Air Canada (Act 1988, privatized 1989) and Petro-Canada (from 1991) confirmed 2026-11-13; unresolved: no primary source fetched for CN's 1995 IPO (only Wikipedia; Justice Laws CN Commercialization Act page not located) -->

> **People disagree about this.** Whether a service should be a Crown corporation is a
> recurring political question. Supporters argue public ownership protects services that
> markets underprovide, like rural mail or public broadcasting, and keeps profits in
> public hands. Critics argue governments run businesses less efficiently, that some
> Crown corporations compete unfairly with private firms, or that public money for
> some (such as the CBC) should be reduced.
