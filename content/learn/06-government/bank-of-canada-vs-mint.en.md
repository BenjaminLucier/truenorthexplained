## In short

The **Bank of Canada** is Canada's **central bank**. It sets the key interest rate to
keep inflation low and stable, issues all banknotes, and helps keep the financial system
working. The **Royal Canadian Mint** is a Crown corporation that manufactures coins,
along with collector coins and gold and silver products. If you're looking for
Canada's version of the US Federal Reserve, it's the Bank of Canada, not the Mint.

## The details

### Side by side

| Main law | *Bank of Canada Act* | *Royal Canadian Mint Act* |
| Began | 1935 (became publicly owned in 1938) | 1908 |
| Makes or issues | **Banknotes** (paper and polymer money) | **Coins** (from 5¢ to the $2 "toonie"), plus collector coins and bullion |
| Sets interest rates? | Yes, the **policy interest rate** | No |
| Controls inflation? | Yes, that's its main job | No |
| Where | Head office in Ottawa | Ottawa and Winnipeg |

### What the Bank of Canada does

The Bank describes five areas of work:

1. **Monetary policy.** Keeping inflation low, stable and predictable, mainly by setting
   the **policy interest rate**. Commercial banks' lending and savings rates tend to
   follow it, which affects borrowing, spending and, eventually, prices. The Bank
   announces rate decisions on scheduled dates through the year.
2. **Financial system.** Promoting a stable and efficient financial system, including
   acting as a lender of last resort in a crisis.
3. **Currency.** Designing, issuing and distributing Canada's **banknotes**.
4. **Funds management.** Acting as the federal government's banker: managing its
   accounts, the public debt and the foreign exchange reserves.
5. **Oversight of payment systems.** Supervising important payment and financial market
   systems.

What it **doesn't** do: it doesn't supervise individual banks for safety (that's the
**Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions**, OSFI), it doesn't decide
taxes or government spending (that's **fiscal policy**, set by the government and
Parliament), and it doesn't lend to the public.

### The inflation target

Every five years, the Bank and the federal government jointly agree on the **monetary
policy framework**. The core of that framework is an
**inflation target**. Targets were introduced in 1991, and since the mid-1990s the target has been 2%, the midpoint of a 1% to 3% range. <!-- check: 1991 introduction; 2% since 1995 --> The Bank then decides,
independently, how to hit it.

> **As of 2026:** In November 2026 the government and the Bank renewed the framework for
> 2027 to 2031, keeping the 2% target. <!-- check: confirm 1–3% range retained in 2026 renewal text -->

### How independent is it?

The Bank is owned by the government, but it runs monetary policy **independently**.
Its **Governor** is appointed by the Bank's independent Board of Directors, with the
federal cabinet's approval, for a seven-year term. The Deputy Minister of Finance sits on
the Board but can't vote.

There is one formal override. Under section 14 of the *Bank of Canada Act*, after
consulting the Governor, the Minister of Finance can issue a **written, public
directive** on monetary policy, which the Bank must follow. This power has never been
used. <!-- check: s. 14 directive never used -->

### What the Mint does

The **Royal Canadian Mint** started in 1908 in Ottawa as a branch of Britain's Royal
Mint and later became Canadian-run. <!-- check: 1931 transfer to Canada; 1969 Crown corporation status --> Today it:

- produces Canada's **circulation coins** and manages the coin system;
- makes **collector coins** and medals;
- makes **bullion** products such as gold and silver investment coins;
- refines precious metals;
- makes coins for other countries.

The *Royal Canadian Mint Act* expects the Mint to make coins on a commercial, for-profit basis. Coins are
**legal tender** under the federal *Currency Act*; the Mint makes them, but the
decisions about what coins Canada uses are made by the government.

Canada stopped distributing the **penny** in 2013. Cash transactions are rounded to the
nearest five cents; electronic payments can still be to the cent.

### Who decides how much money there is?

Mostly neither of them directly. Banknotes and coins are a small share of the money in
the economy; most money is deposits in bank accounts, created when banks make loans.
The Bank of Canada influences how much borrowing happens by setting interest rates.
The Mint and the Bank only produce as much cash as people and businesses want to hold.

### The constitutional link

Under s. 91 of the Constitution Act, 1867, **currency and coinage** (s. 91(14)),
**banking and the issue of paper money** (s. 91(15)) and **legal tender** (s. 91(20))
are all federal. That's why there's one national currency and provinces can't issue
their own. See [the division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers).
