## In short

**Officers of Parliament** (also called **agents of Parliament**) are independent
watchdogs created by law to help Parliament keep an eye on the government. They report
directly to Parliament, not to a minister, and they're hard to fire. The best known is
the **Auditor General**, who checks how public money is spent. Their power is mostly
the power to investigate and publish; it's up to Parliament and the public to act on
what they find.

## The details

### What makes someone an officer of Parliament?

There's no single legal definition. The Library of Parliament describes four common
features:

1. **Parliament is involved in the appointment**, usually by consultation with party
   leaders and a vote in the House, the [Senate](org:senate) or both.
2. **Parliament is involved in removal**, so the government can't fire them on its own.
3. A **fixed term** set in law.
4. They **report directly to Parliament**.

They're also independent of Parliament itself: committees can study their reports but
can't direct their work.

### The nine officers

| Officer | Watches over |
|---|---|
| **Auditor General of Canada** | How the government manages and spends public money |
| **Parliamentary Budget Officer** | Budgets, estimates and the cost of proposals |
| **Conflict of Interest and Ethics Commissioner** | Conflicts of interest among ministers, MPs and senior appointees |
| **Chief Electoral Officer** | Federal elections (heads [Elections Canada](org:elections-canada)) |
| **Commissioner of Official Languages** | Compliance with the *Official Languages Act* |
| **Privacy Commissioner** | Privacy law, for government and private businesses |
| **Information Commissioner** | Access to government records |
| **Commissioner of Lobbying** | The registry and code of conduct for lobbyists |
| **Public Sector Integrity Commissioner** | Disclosures of wrongdoing (whistleblowing) in the public sector |

### The Auditor General

The **Auditor General** audits the federal government and reports to Parliament. Its
work includes:

- **Financial audits:** checking that the government's financial statements (the
  **[Public Accounts of Canada](term:public-accounts-of-canada)**) and those of many Crown corporations are accurate.
- **Performance audits:** checking whether programs are well managed and achieve
  what they're supposed to, with due regard for economy and efficiency. These are the
  reports that tend to make the news.
- **Environmental audits**, through the **Commissioner of the Environment and
  Sustainable Development**, who works inside the Auditor General's office.

The Auditor General also audits the three territorial governments for their
legislatures.

Under the *Auditor General Act*, the Auditor General is appointed by [cabinet](org:cabinet) only after
consulting the leader of every recognized party in the House of Commons and the Senate
leaders, and the appointment must be approved by resolution of both houses. The term is
**ten years** and can't be renewed, and removal for cause requires a [joint address](term:joint-address) of
the Senate and House. That's designed so no single government controls the office.

Audit reports are studied by the House of Commons **[Public Accounts Committee](term:public-accounts-committee)**, which
is chaired by a member of the [Official Opposition](term:official-opposition). <!-- check: Standing Order 106(2) -->

### The Parliamentary Budget Officer

The **Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO)** gives MPs and senators independent analysis of
the economy, the government's finances and the estimates. MPs and senators can ask the
PBO to estimate the cost of a proposal. Before a general election, political parties
and independent MPs can also ask the PBO to cost their campaign promises.

The office was created in 2006 and was made an independent officer of Parliament in
2017. <!-- check: 2006 creation (Federal Accountability Act) and 2017 status change -->

### Ethics

The **Conflict of Interest and Ethics Commissioner** administers two sets of rules:

- the ***Conflict of Interest Act***, for ministers, [parliamentary secretaries](term:parliamentary-secretary) and many
  public office holders appointed by cabinet or ministers; and
- the **Conflict of Interest Code for Members of the House of Commons**, for MPs.

Ministers are covered by both. **Senators** have their own separate **[Senate Ethics
Officer](term:senate-ethics-officer)** and code.

### What they can and can't do

Mostly, officers of Parliament **investigate, report and recommend**. They can't
overturn a government decision or fine a minister for a bad program. Their influence
comes from publicity and from Parliament's response. There are exceptions with sharper
teeth: for example, the Information Commissioner can order departments to release
records, and the Commissioner of Canada Elections (part of the Chief Electoral
Officer's organization) can enforce election law. <!-- check: Information Commissioner order-making power since 2019 -->

### Not to be confused with

- **Ombudspersons** for particular groups, like the Veterans Ombud or the Taxpayers'
  Ombudsperson, usually report to a minister, not to Parliament.
  <!-- check: reporting relationships of federal ombuds -->
- **Provincial watchdogs.** Each province has its own auditor general, ethics or
  integrity commissioner, and in most provinces an ombudsperson who reports to the
  provincial legislature.
