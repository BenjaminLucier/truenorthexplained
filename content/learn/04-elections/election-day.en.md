## In short

A federal election starts when the Governor General, on the Prime Minister's advice,
**dissolves** Parliament and the cabinet orders **writs** (the formal orders to hold an
election) for every riding. The campaign lasts between 36 and 50 days. You can vote on
election day, at advance polls, or by special ballot, including by mail. **Elections Canada**,
an independent agency, runs the whole thing. Federal law sets a "fixed" election date, but the
Prime Minister can still advise an earlier election, and several have happened.

## The details

### Step by step

1. **Dissolution.** The Prime Minister asks the Governor General to dissolve Parliament. By
   convention, the Governor General normally agrees. All seats in the House become vacant.
2. **Writs issued.** The cabinet issues a proclamation, and the Chief Electoral Officer
   issues a writ for each riding. Election day must be between the 36th and 50th day after
   the writs are issued, and is normally a Monday.
3. **The campaign.** Parties nominate candidates, campaign, and must follow spending and
   advertising rules in the Canada Elections Act.
4. **Advance polls.** Held on the 10th, 9th, 8th and 7th days before election day.
5. **Election day.** Polls are open for 12 hours, with times staggered by time zone so that
   most close at around the same moment across the country.
6. **Counting.** Paper ballots are counted by hand at each polling station after polls close,
   with candidates' representatives allowed to watch.
7. **Validation and recounts.** The **returning officer** (the official running the election
   in each riding) checks the results. If the gap between the top two candidates is less than
   **1/1,000** of the votes cast, a judge holds an automatic recount.
8. **The new House meets.** The new MPs are sworn in and Parliament opens with a Speech from
   the Throne.

### How you vote

**Who can vote:** any Canadian citizen who is 18 or older on election day.

**Ways to vote:**

| Option | When | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Election day** | Polling day | At your assigned polling station |
| **Advance polls** | 10th to 7th day before election day | At your assigned advance polling station |
| **Special ballot** | After the election is called, before a deadline | At any Elections Canada office, or by mail, including from abroad |

Once you register to vote by special ballot, you can't switch to voting in person.

**ID:** you have to prove both **who you are** and **where you live**. Many kinds of document
are accepted. If you don't have them, you can declare your identity and address in writing
and have another voter from your polling division vouch for you.

**Time off work:** employees are entitled to **three consecutive hours** to vote while polls
are open. If their shift doesn't allow it, the employer has to give time off, at a time the
employer chooses, without cutting pay (Canada Elections Act, s. 132).

### Ridings and their boundaries

Each **riding** (officially an **electoral district**) elects one MP. After each 10-year
census, independent commissions in each province redraw riding boundaries so they reflect
population changes. The number of seats per province is set by a formula in the Constitution
and federal law.

> **As of 2026:** There are 343 ridings, in effect since the 2025 general election.

The [ridings map](/ridings) shows all 343, with each one's 2025 result, and can find the
riding you live in.

The formula starts from population, but the Constitution also guarantees smaller and slower-growing
provinces a minimum number of seats, so seat shares don't match population shares exactly.

### Fixed election dates, and why they aren't really fixed

In 2007, Parliament amended the Canada Elections Act to set a **fixed election date**. Under
section 56.1, a general election is to be held on the **third Monday of October in the fourth
calendar year** after the previous election. The Chief Electoral Officer can recommend moving
it by a day or a week if the date clashes with something like a religious holiday or a
provincial election (s. 56.2).

But the same section begins by saying that nothing in it affects the **Governor General's
powers, including the power to dissolve Parliament** at the Governor General's discretion.
And by convention, the Governor General acts on the Prime Minister's advice.

So in practice:

| Year | What happened |
|---|---|
| 2008 | An election was called before the first fixed date. A court challenge argued this broke the law. The Federal Court and Federal Court of Appeal disagreed: the law didn't limit the power to advise dissolution. |
| 2015 | Held on the fixed date. |
| 2019 | Held on the fixed date. |
| 2021 | Called early. A similar court challenge was again dismissed. |
| 2025 | Called early. |

A government also can't use the fixed date to avoid an election if it loses a
[confidence vote](/learn/parliament/confidence). The fixed date works as a default and an
outside expectation, not a guarantee.

**People disagree about this.** Some argue fixed dates should be binding so governments can't
time elections for advantage. Others argue flexibility is essential in a parliamentary system,
especially in a minority Parliament, where the House may stop working.

Most provinces and territories have their own fixed-date laws, with similar exceptions.

### Elections Canada

**Elections Canada** is the non-partisan agency that runs federal elections, by-elections
and referendums. It's headed by the **Chief Electoral Officer**, who reports to Parliament,
not to the government. It also registers parties, enforces political financing rules and
publishes results.

A **by-election** fills a single seat that becomes vacant between general elections, for
example when an MP resigns or dies.
