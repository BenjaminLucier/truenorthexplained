## In short

Each federal party chooses its own leader under its own rules, usually through a vote of
party members. Anyone who joins the party by the deadline can typically take part, but the
general public doesn't vote. Because the Prime Minister is normally the leader of the
governing party, a party leadership race can change the Prime Minister without a general
election. Since 2015, federal law also lets each party's MPs vote on whether they can
trigger a review of their leader.

## The details

### Why leaders matter so much

The [Prime Minister](/learn/elections) is whoever can keep the House's [confidence](term:confidence), which in
practice means the leader of the governing party. Party leaders also:

- lead their party's election campaign and appear in televised debates;
- decide, in the governing party, who sits in [cabinet](org:cabinet);
- in opposition parties, choose their party's critics (spokespeople) and set its strategy;
- often control who can run under the party's name. Under the [Canada Elections Act](doc:canada-elections-act), each
  party tells [](org:elections-canada) who is authorized to endorse its candidates (s. 406(2)), and
  in many parties that includes the leader.

### How leaders are chosen today

Parties write their own leadership rules. Federal law regulates the **money** (leadership
candidates have to register and follow contribution and reporting rules under the Canada
Elections Act) but not the voting method.

In most major federal parties today, the process looks something like this:

1. **The race is called** after a leader resigns, dies or loses a review.
2. **Candidates enter**, usually paying a fee and collecting signatures from members.
3. **People join the party** to vote. Membership is usually cheap or free and open to most
   residents, sometimes including people who aren't citizens or are under 18, depending on
   party rules. <!-- check: membership eligibility varies by party; confirm before giving specifics; unresolved 2026-11-13: party membership rules not reachable (pages 404 or unparsed) and web search unavailable -->
4. **Members vote**, often by **[ranked ballot](term:ranked-ballot)**, so a winner emerges with majority support
   after lower-ranked candidates are eliminated.
5. Some parties **weight votes by riding**, so each riding counts equally no matter how many
   members it has. Others count every member's vote equally.

Historically, leaders were chosen at **delegate conventions**, where local party associations
sent delegates to vote in person. Most parties have since moved to direct votes by all
members.

### Leadership reviews

Most parties also hold periodic **leadership reviews**, often at a party convention after an
election loss, where members vote on whether to keep the leader. A poor result can push a
leader to resign even if they technically survive.

### The caucus rules in the Parliament of Canada Act

A 2015 amendment to the **[Parliament of Canada Act](term:parliament-of-canada-act)** (often called the **Reform Act**) gave
MPs some formal powers within their party's **[caucus](term:caucus)** (a party's group of MPs). After every
general election, at its first meeting, each party caucus with **12 or more MPs** must hold
four separate recorded votes on whether to adopt these rules:

| Rule | What it allows |
|---|---|
| Expulsion and readmission | Removing an MP from caucus needs a written request from 20% of caucus and a secret-ballot majority of all caucus members. |
| Caucus chair | The caucus elects its own chair, and can remove the chair. |
| Leadership review | A written request signed by 20% of caucus triggers a secret-ballot vote on whether to keep the leader. A majority vote can remove the leader. |
| Interim leader | If the leader is removed or leaves, the caucus elects an interim leader until the party picks a permanent one. |

Each rule applies only if a majority of the caucus votes for it, and the result lasts until
the next dissolution of Parliament. Caucuses have voted different ways on these questions.

### Changing the Prime Minister between elections

When the governing party picks a new leader, that person becomes Prime Minister without a
general election. The outgoing Prime Minister resigns, and the [Governor General](org:governor-general) appoints the
new leader. This has happened several times in Canada's history, including in 1993, 2003 and
2025.

A new leader doesn't need a seat in the House to become Prime Minister. By convention, a
Prime Minister without a seat is expected to win one in a [by-election](term:by-election) or the next general
election.

### Provinces work the same way

Provincial parties choose their leaders the same way, and the leader of the governing party
becomes **[premier](term:premier)**. A change of premier between elections is common.
