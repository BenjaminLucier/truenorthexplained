## In short

Canada elects MPs with **first-past-the-post (FPTP)**: in each [riding](term:riding), the candidate with the
most votes wins, even if that's well short of half. It's simple and gives every area one
clear local MP. It also means a party's share of seats can be very different from its share
of votes. Several alternatives have been proposed, and a [House of Commons](org:house-of-commons) committee studied
them in 2016, but the federal system hasn't changed.

## The details

### How it works

1. The country is divided into ridings. Each elects one MP.
2. Voters mark **one** candidate.
3. The candidate with the **most votes** (a **[plurality](term:plurality)**) wins. There's no runoff and no
   minimum share.
4. Votes for losing candidates don't carry over anywhere.

Political scientists call this a **single-member plurality** system. The name comes from horse
racing: there's a "post" (the winning line), and the first one past it wins.

### A worked example

Imagine a riding with three candidates:

| Candidate | Votes | Share |
|---|---|---|
| A | 18,000 | 36% |
| B | 17,000 | 34% |
| C | 15,000 | 30% |

Candidate A wins, even though 64% of voters chose someone else.

This isn't only a textbook case. In real elections, many ridings are won with less than half

### What it does to results

Because each riding is a separate winner-take-all contest, national seat totals can differ a
lot from national vote shares:

- **"Manufactured" majorities.** A party can win a majority of seats with well under half the
  vote. In 2015, the winning party formed a majority government with about 39.5% of the vote.
- **Most votes, fewer seats.** In 2019 and 2021, the party that won the most votes nationally
  won fewer seats than another party, which went on to form the government.
- **Regional concentration helps.** A party whose voters are concentrated in one region can
  win many seats there. A party with spread-out support can come second in many ridings and
  win few seats.
- **[Strategic voting](term:strategic-voting).** Some voters choose a candidate they think can win rather than their
  first choice, to avoid "splitting the vote."

### The main alternatives

| System | How it works | Main argument for | Main argument against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First-past-the-post** (current) | One MP per riding; most votes wins | Simple; clear local MP; often produces majority governments | Seats can differ sharply from votes |
| **[Ranked ballot](term:ranked-ballot)** (alternative vote) | One MP per riding; voters rank candidates; lowest are eliminated and their votes transferred until someone has a majority | Winner has majority support after transfers; keeps local ridings | Seat totals can still differ sharply from votes |
| **[Mixed-member proportional](term:mixed-member-proportional) (MMP)** | Some MPs elected in ridings; extra "list" MPs added so each party's total seats match its vote share | Seats roughly match votes; keeps local MPs | Two kinds of MP; larger ridings; parties control lists |
| **[Single transferable vote](term:single-transferable-vote) (STV)** | Larger ridings with several MPs each; voters rank candidates | More proportional; voters choose individuals, not lists | Complex counting; very large ridings in rural areas |
| **[List proportional representation](term:list-proportional-representation)** | Voters choose parties; seats shared by vote share | Closely proportional | No local MP; parties choose who sits |

Proportional systems tend to produce more minority and [coalition](term:coalition) governments. Supporters
see this as encouraging cooperation. Critics see it as giving small parties too much
leverage and making governments less stable.

### The 2016 Special Committee on Electoral Reform

In the 2015 campaign, the party that went on to form government promised that 2015 would be
the last federal election held under first-past-the-post.

- In **June 2016**, the House of Commons created a **Special Committee on Electoral Reform**,
  with members from all parties.
- It held hearings across the country and received submissions from experts and the public.
- In **December 2016**, it tabled its report, *Strengthening Democracy in Canada: Principles,
  Process and Public Engagement for Electoral Reform*. Among other things, it recommended that
  the government hold a **[referendum](term:referendum)** offering a proportional system, with the current system
  also on the ballot. It recommended against mandatory voting and online voting "at this
  time." Some committee members filed dissenting or supplementary opinions.
- In **February 2017**, the government announced it would not pursue a change to the electoral
  system, saying there was no clear consensus on an alternative.

**People disagree about this.** Supporters of reform say first-past-the-post wastes many votes
and gives majorities to parties most voters didn't choose, and that the promise should have
been kept. Supporters of the current system say it is simple, keeps a strong local link and
produces stable governments, and that a change this big shouldn't happen without broad
consensus or a referendum. Others favour a ranked ballot as a middle option.

### Provinces have tried too

Several provinces have held referendums on changing their own electoral systems, including
British Columbia, Ontario and Prince Edward Island. None has led to a change so far.

> **As of 2026:** Federal elections use first-past-the-post. Wikipedia's article on
> elections in Canada says every province and territory uses it too.
