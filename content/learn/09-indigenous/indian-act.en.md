## In short

The **Indian Act** is a federal law, first passed in 1876, that governs many parts of
life for **First Nations** people who are registered under it. It defines who has
**status**, how **reserves** work, and how **band councils** are set up. For much of its
history it was a tool of assimilation, and it was the legal backbone of the residential
school system. It still applies today. Many people want it gone, but replacing it is
hard because so much depends on it.

## The details

### Where it comes from

Section 91(24) of the Constitution Act, 1867 gives Parliament power over "Indians, and
Lands reserved for the Indians." In 1876, Parliament used that power to pull earlier
colonial laws together into one statute, the *Indian Act*. It has been amended many
times, most heavily in 1951 and 1985, but it has never been replaced.

The Act applies to **First Nations** people and communities. It doesn't cover **Inuit**
or **Métis**, even though courts have held that both fall under federal jurisdiction
(see [Section 35](/learn/indigenous/section-35)).

### Status

**Status** (or being a **registered Indian**) is the legal standing of a person who is
registered under the Indian Act. The federal government keeps the list, called the
**Indian Register**. Status carries certain rights and benefits, such as some health
benefits and a tax exemption on certain property on reserve.

Status isn't the same as identity:

- A **non-status** First Nations person is someone who identifies as First Nations
  but isn't registered, often because of past rules in the Act.
- **Band membership** (belonging to a particular First Nation) is separate from status.
  Many First Nations now control their own membership lists.
- There's no federal register for **Métis** or **Inuit**. Métis governments and Inuit
  land claim organizations keep their own.

For over a century, the Act took status away from people for various reasons. Most
notoriously, a First Nations woman who married a man without status **lost her own
status**, and so did her children. A man who married a woman without status kept his,
and his wife gained it. Sandra Lovelace, a Wolastoqiyik woman from New Brunswick, took
this to the UN Human Rights Committee, which found against Canada in 1981. In 1985,
**Bill C-31** removed the rule and restored status to many people. Later changes,
prompted by court cases, followed in 2011 and 2017.

### Reserves

A **reserve** is land set apart for the use and benefit of a **band**. Legal title stays
with the Crown, not the First Nation. That's why reserve land generally can't be
mortgaged or sold the way other land can, which affects housing and business. Reserves
are often small fractions of a nation's traditional territory.

Not all First Nations people live on reserves, and Métis and Inuit communities aren't
reserves at all.

### Band councils

The Act created the **band council**: an elected chief and councillors who run the
reserve community, with powers set by the Act. For decades, many council decisions
needed the approval of the minister responsible for Indian affairs.

Today, First Nations can choose among Indian Act elections, **custom** election codes
designed by the community, or elections under the *First Nations Elections Act* (2014).

Band councils sit alongside, and sometimes in tension with, **traditional governments**
such as hereditary chiefs and clan systems. These existed long before the Act. The Act
imposed the band council model, and the Canadian government only recognized the
council. In some nations, such as the Wet'suwet'en, the elected council and the
hereditary chiefs have at times taken different positions on major issues.

### Assimilation and its tools

For much of its history, the Act aimed to absorb First Nations people into settler
society. At various times it:

- **banned ceremonies**, such as the potlatch and certain dances (a ban added in 1884
  and removed in 1951)
- stopped First Nations from hiring lawyers to pursue land claims without government
  approval (1927 to 1951) <!-- check: wording of 1927 restriction -->
- used **enfranchisement**: a status person who became a lawyer or a university
  graduate, for example, could lose status. It was presented as a path to citizenship
  and the vote.
- made attendance at **residential schools** mandatory for many children (from 1920)

First Nations people gained the right to vote in federal elections without losing
status in 1960.

### Residential schools, briefly

**Residential schools** were boarding schools for Indigenous children, funded by the
federal government and mostly run by churches. They operated from the 1800s until the
last one closed in the 1990s. More than 150,000 First Nations, Inuit and Métis children
attended, many removed from their families by force. Children were punished for
speaking their languages, many suffered abuse, and thousands died.

After a class action settlement, the **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)** was
created in 2008. In 2015 it published its findings, describing the system as part of a
policy of **cultural genocide**, and issued **94 Calls to Action**. The federal
government formally apologized in 2008. Records are kept by the **National Centre for
Truth and Reconciliation** at the University of Manitoba.

### Why not just repeal it?

Because the Act is deeply woven into daily life. Reserve land, status, band governments
and many programs all depend on it. Repealing it overnight would leave a legal vacuum.
Many First Nations also worry that repeal could be used to reduce federal obligations.
In 1969, a federal White Paper proposed abolishing the Act and status entirely.
First Nations rejected it strongly, seeing it as assimilation by another name, and it
was withdrawn. <!-- check: 1969 White Paper withdrawn 1971 -->

The main route out today is **opting out piece by piece**: through
[self-government agreements](/learn/indigenous/self-government), modern treaties, and
laws that let First Nations take over areas such as land management.
