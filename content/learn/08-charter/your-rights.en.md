## In short

The Charter's rights fall into seven groups: fundamental freedoms, democratic rights,
mobility rights, legal rights, equality rights, official language rights and minority
language education rights. Most belong to everyone in Canada; a few belong only to
citizens. All of them protect you against **government**, not against private people or
businesses ([s. 32](doc:charter/s32)). This page paraphrases each section. For the exact words, read the
[Charter itself](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/page-12.html).

## The details

### Fundamental freedoms (s. 2)

**Everyone** has:

- **(a) freedom of conscience and religion:** to hold beliefs, or none, and practise them
- **(b) freedom of thought, belief, opinion and expression,** including freedom of the
  press and other media
- **(c) freedom of peaceful assembly:** to gather, for example at a protest
- **(d) freedom of association:** to form and join groups, such as unions

### Democratic rights (ss. 3–5)

- **s. 3:** Every **citizen** can vote in federal and provincial elections, and can run
  for a seat in the House of Commons or a provincial legislature.
- **s. 4:** The House of Commons and each provincial legislature can't go more than
  **five years** without an election. The only exception is during real or feared war,
  invasion or insurrection, and then only if no more than one third of the members
  oppose the extension.
- **s. 5:** Parliament and each legislature must sit at least once every **12 months**.

### Mobility rights (s. 6)

- **s. 6(1):** Every **citizen** can enter, stay in and leave Canada.
- **s. 6(2):** Citizens and **permanent residents** can move to and live in any province,
  and earn a living there.
- Provinces can still apply general laws to everyone, and can set reasonable residency
  periods before newcomers qualify for some public social services. A province whose
  employment rate is below the national rate can also run programs that favour its
  socially or economically disadvantaged residents.

### Legal rights (ss. 7–14)

These matter most when you deal with police or the criminal justice system. They belong
to **everyone**, unless noted.

| Section | Plain language |
|---|---|
| **s. 7** | The right to **life, liberty and security of the person**, and not to lose them except in line with the **[principles of fundamental justice](term:principles-of-fundamental-justice)** (basic fairness) |
| **s. 8** | Protection against **unreasonable search or seizure** |
| **s. 9** | The right not to be **arbitrarily detained or imprisoned** |
| **s. 10** | If you're arrested or detained: to be told **promptly why**, to **get a lawyer without delay and be told you have that right**, and to have a court check whether your detention is lawful (*[habeas corpus](term:habeas-corpus)*) |
| **s. 11** | If you're **charged with an offence**: to be told the charge without unreasonable delay; to be **tried within a reasonable time**; not to be forced to testify against yourself in your own trial; to be **[presumed innocent](term:presumption-of-innocence)** until proven guilty in a fair, public hearing by an independent and impartial court; not to be denied reasonable bail without just cause; to a **jury trial** if the maximum penalty is five years or more (except under military law); not to be convicted for something that wasn't an offence when you did it; not to be tried or punished twice for the same offence; and to the lighter punishment if the penalty changed between the offence and sentencing |
| **s. 12** | Protection against **cruel and unusual** treatment or punishment |
| **s. 13** | If you testify, what you say can't be used to incriminate you in other proceedings, except in a prosecution for perjury (lying under oath) or for giving contradictory evidence |
| **s. 14** | The right to an **interpreter** in legal proceedings if you don't understand or speak the language being used, or are deaf |

### Equality rights (s. 15)

- **s. 15(1):** Every individual is **equal before and under the law** and has the right to
  the equal protection and benefit of the law, without discrimination. The Charter lists
  race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, and mental or physical
  disability. Courts have added other, similar grounds, such as **sexual orientation**,
  marital status and citizenship.
- **s. 15(2):** Programs designed to improve the situation of disadvantaged individuals or
  groups (for example, employment equity programs) are allowed.

Section 15 came into force on April 17, **1985**, three years after the rest of the
Charter, to give governments time to bring their laws into line (s. 32(2)).

### Official languages (ss. 16–22)

- **English and French** are the [official languages](term:official-languages) of **Canada** and of **New
  Brunswick**, with equal status in their institutions (s. 16). The English-speaking and
  French-speaking communities of New Brunswick also have equal status (s. 16.1).
- **Anyone** may use English or French in debates in Parliament and in the New Brunswick
  legislature (s. 17), and in federal courts and New Brunswick courts (s. 19).
- Federal laws and records, and New Brunswick's, are published in **both languages**
  (s. 18).
- Members of the public can communicate with and get services from federal
  institutions in English or French, at head offices and wherever there is significant
  demand (s. 20). New Brunswick's institutions must serve the public in both languages.
- These sections don't take away language rights that exist elsewhere, including
  Quebec's and Manitoba's existing constitutional language guarantees (ss. 21–22).

### Minority language education rights (s. 23)

**Citizens** who belong to the English-speaking minority in Quebec, or the
French-speaking minority elsewhere, can have their children educated in that language,
using public funds, **where numbers warrant**. This can include the right to separate
schools and to some control over them.

Who qualifies depends on the parent's first language learned and still understood, the
language of the parent's own primary schooling in Canada, or the language a child has
already been schooled in. One of these tests (the parent's first language) doesn't yet
apply in Quebec (s. 59 of the [Constitution Act, 1982](doc:constitution-act-1982)).

### The rest of the Charter

| Section | What it does |
|---|---|
| s. 1 | Rights are subject to reasonable limits. See [Reasonable limits](doc:charter/s1). |
| s. 24 | Courts can give remedies for breaches, and can exclude evidence obtained in breach of the Charter |
| s. 25 | The Charter doesn't reduce Aboriginal, treaty or other rights of Aboriginal peoples. See [Indigenous peoples](/learn/indigenous). |
| s. 27 | The Charter is to be interpreted in a way that preserves and enhances Canada's multicultural heritage |
| s. 28 | All Charter rights are guaranteed equally to men and women |
| s. 29 | Existing constitutional rights of denominational (religious) schools are preserved |
| s. 32 | The Charter applies to Parliament, the provincial legislatures and governments |
| s. 33 | The notwithstanding clause. See [The notwithstanding clause](doc:charter/s33). |

### Who gets which rights

| Belongs to | Rights |
|---|---|
| Citizens only | Voting and running for office (s. 3), entering and leaving Canada (s. 6(1)), minority language education (s. 23) |
| Citizens and permanent residents | Moving to and working in any province (s. 6(2)) |
| Everyone / every individual | Fundamental freedoms, legal rights, equality rights |

Some rights, like freedom of expression, can be claimed by corporations too. Others,
such as the section 7 right to life, liberty and security of the person, have been held
to protect only human beings. The Supreme Court hasn't squarely decided whether
corporations have section 15 equality rights, but its reasoning suggests they don't.
