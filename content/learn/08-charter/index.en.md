## TL;DR

The **Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms** is part of the Constitution. Since 1982
it has protected rights like freedom of expression and religion, the right to vote,
legal rights when you're arrested or charged, and equality. It protects you from
**governments**, not from other people or private businesses. Rights aren't absolute:
under **section 1**, a government can limit a right if it can show the limit is
reasonable and justified. And under **section 33**, the **notwithstanding clause**,
Parliament or a provincial legislature can override some rights for up to five years at
a time. Courts enforce the Charter, and can strike down laws that break it.

## Core

### What it is

The Charter is the first part of the **Constitution Act, 1982**, which came into force on
April 17, 1982, when Canada [patriated its Constitution](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982).
Because it's part of the Constitution, it's **supreme law**: an ordinary law that
conflicts with it can be struck down.

Before 1982, Canada had the *Canadian Bill of Rights* (1960). But that was an ordinary
federal law. It applied only to federal laws, and courts rarely used it to strike
anything down. The Charter changed that: it applies to every level of government, and
courts enforce it.

### What it protects

The Charter groups rights into families. Here's the short version; see
[Your Charter rights](/learn/charter/your-rights) for each section in plain language.

| Group | Sections | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental freedoms | s. 2 | Religion, expression, peaceful assembly, association |
| Democratic rights | ss. 3–5 | Citizens' right to vote and run for office; elections at least every five years |
| Mobility rights | s. 6 | Enter and leave Canada; live and work in any province |
| Legal rights | ss. 7–14 | Life, liberty and security of the person; no unreasonable search; a lawyer on arrest; presumed innocent |
| Equality rights | s. 15 | Equal treatment under the law, without discrimination |
| Official languages | ss. 16–22 | English and French in Parliament, federal courts and federal services |
| Minority language education | s. 23 | Schooling in English in Quebec or French elsewhere, where numbers warrant |

Some rights belong to **everyone** in Canada, including visitors and non-citizens (for
example, the legal rights). Others belong only to **citizens** (voting, the right to
enter Canada, minority language education rights).

### Who it binds: governments only

Section 32 says the Charter applies to Parliament and the federal government, and to
the provincial legislatures and governments. That includes everything they do:
laws, regulations, policies, and actions by officials such as police officers and
border agents. Cities are covered too, because their powers come from the provinces.

It **doesn't** apply to private people or private businesses. If a private employer or
landlord treats you unfairly, the Charter isn't the tool. Instead, federal and
provincial **human rights laws** protect people against discrimination in things like
jobs, housing and services.

### Rights have limits: section 1

The very first section says the Charter's rights are guaranteed "subject only to such
reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and
democratic society."

That means a court asks two questions:

1. **Does this law or action limit a Charter right?** The person challenging it has to
   show that.
2. **If so, is the limit justified?** Now it's the **government's** job to prove the limit
   is reasonable, using a test from the 1986 case *R. v. Oakes*: an important goal,
   sensible means, as little harm to the right as reasonably possible, and benefits that
   outweigh the costs.

Hate speech laws are an example: they limit freedom of expression, but courts have found
them justified. [More on section 1 and the Oakes test](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits).

### The override: section 33

Section 33, the **notwithstanding clause**, lets Parliament or a provincial legislature
pass a law that operates "notwithstanding" certain Charter rights: fundamental freedoms
(s. 2), legal rights (ss. 7–14) and equality (s. 15). It can't be used on democratic,
mobility or language rights. Each use expires after **five years** unless it's passed
again.

It was a compromise in 1981 to get most provinces to agree to the Charter. The federal
Parliament has never used it. Several provinces (and, once, Yukon) have, and its use is one of the most
debated questions in Canadian politics.
[More on the notwithstanding clause](/learn/charter/notwithstanding-clause).

### How the Charter gets enforced

If you believe a government has violated your Charter rights, you can raise it in court.
Section 24 lets courts give a remedy that is "appropriate and just". In a criminal case,
evidence obtained by breaking your rights can be excluded if using it would bring the
justice system into disrepute. And under section 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982, a law
that breaks the Charter is of no force or effect.

Courts don't have the last word on everything. Legislatures often respond to a court
ruling by passing a revised law, and courts generally give governments some room to
choose among reasonable options. Some people call this back-and-forth a **dialogue**
between courts and legislatures.

### People disagree about this

The Charter itself is widely popular, but people disagree about how it should be used:

- Some argue that unelected judges have too much power to overturn decisions made by
  elected legislatures, and that the notwithstanding clause is a legitimate check.
- Others argue that protecting minorities from majority decisions is exactly what
  courts are for, and that overriding rights should be rare or never pre-emptive.

## Misconceptions

**"The Charter protects me from my employer, my landlord or a store."**
No. The Charter applies only to governments and to bodies acting as government. Private
discrimination is covered by federal and provincial human rights laws instead.

**"Charter rights are absolute."**
No. Section 1 allows reasonable limits that a government can justify, and section 33
lets legislatures override some rights for five years at a time.

**"The notwithstanding clause can override any right."**
No. It can't touch democratic rights, mobility rights, or language rights, including
minority language education rights. It covers only sections 2 and 7 to 15.

**"Only Canadian citizens have Charter rights."**
Most Charter rights belong to "everyone" or "any person" in Canada, including
non-citizens. Only a few, such as voting, the right to enter Canada and minority
language education rights, are limited to citizens.

**"Freedom of speech in Canada works the same as in the US."**
Not exactly. Freedom of expression is strongly protected, but under section 1 courts
have upheld some limits that might not survive in the US, such as criminal laws against
hate propaganda.
