## In short

Section 33 lets Parliament or a provincial legislature declare that a law will operate
**notwithstanding** (that is, despite) certain Charter rights: the fundamental freedoms in
section 2, the legal rights in sections 7 to 14, and the equality rights in section 15.
The declaration expires after **five years** unless the legislature passes it again. It
can't be used on democratic, mobility or language rights. The federal Parliament has
never used it, but several provinces have, including in recent years.

## The details

### What it says

Section 33(1) says Parliament or a provincial legislature may "expressly declare" in a
law that the law, or part of it, "shall operate notwithstanding a provision included in
section 2 or sections 7 to 15" of the Charter.

In practice:

1. A legislature puts a clause in a bill saying it applies notwithstanding those
   sections.
2. Once passed, courts can't strike the law down for violating the rights named.
3. After **five years** (or sooner, if the law says so), the declaration expires.
4. The legislature can **re-enact** it, for another five years at most, as many times as
   it likes.

The five-year limit matches the longest time a legislature can go without an election
(s. 4). The idea is that voters get a chance to weigh in before an override is renewed.

### What it can and can't override

| Can be overridden | Can't be overridden |
|---|---|
| Fundamental freedoms (s. 2): religion, expression, assembly, association | Democratic rights (ss. 3–5): voting, maximum five-year terms, annual sittings |
| Legal rights (ss. 7–14): life, liberty and security of the person, search and seizure, rights on arrest and at trial | Mobility rights (s. 6) |
| Equality rights (s. 15) | Official language rights (ss. 16–22) |
| | Minority language education rights (s. 23) |

It also can't be used to override the Constitution's other parts, such as the division of
powers between Ottawa and the provinces. A province still can't use section 33 to pass a
law on a federal subject like criminal law.

### What courts have said about how it's used

In *Ford v. Quebec (Attorney General)* (1988), the Supreme Court said section 33 sets a
requirement of **form only**:

- The legislature doesn't have to explain or justify the override.
- It's enough to refer to the Charter sections being overridden by their numbers. (If
  only part of a section is being overridden, the law has to say which part in words.)
- An override can't be **retroactive**. It can only apply going forward.

> **As of 2026:** Whether a court can still examine a law that uses section 33, and
> declare that it limits Charter rights even though the law stays in force, is an open
> and actively litigated question.

### Why it exists

During the 1981 negotiations over [patriation](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), several
provinces didn't want an entrenched Charter that would let courts overrule elected
legislatures. The notwithstanding clause was part of the November 1981 compromise
between the federal government and nine provinces that made the Charter possible. Quebec
did not agree to the deal.

### How it's been used

> [!NOTE]
> **The federal Parliament has never used section 33.**

Provinces and territories that have used it include Quebec, Saskatchewan, Ontario,
Alberta and Yukon. Some of these laws never actually came into force. Several provinces
have used it in recent years. Some examples, oldest first:

Some recent uses have been **pre-emptive**: the override was included before any court
had ruled on whether the law breached the Charter.

### People disagree about this

The notwithstanding clause is one of the most debated parts of the Constitution.

- **Supporters** say it keeps the final word on important social questions with elected
  legislatures, who answer to voters, rather than unelected judges. They point out it was
  a deliberate part of the 1981 deal, and that the five-year limit keeps it accountable.
- **Critics** say rights are meant to protect minorities from majorities, so letting a
  majority override them defeats the purpose. Many especially object to pre-emptive use,
  which can stop courts from hearing a challenge at all.
- **In between,** some argue it's legitimate but should be rare: used only after a court
  ruling, and only after public debate.

Changing or removing section 33 itself would require a constitutional amendment. See
[the amending formula](/learn/constitution/amending-formula).
