## In short

Only Parliament can make **criminal law**, so the **[Criminal Code](term:criminal-code)** is the same from coast
to coast. But the provinces are in charge of the **administration of justice**: they run
most courts, most police forces and most prosecutions. Provinces can also create their
own **provincial offences**, like speeding, but those aren't crimes in the
constitutional sense.

## The details

### The constitutional split

| Who | Power | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Parliament | Criminal law, and the procedure in criminal matters | Constitution Act, 1867, s. 91(27) |
| Parliament | Penitentiaries (federal prisons) | s. 91(28) |
| Provinces | Administration of justice, including setting up and running courts | s. 92(14) |
| Provinces | Fines, penalties or imprisonment to enforce their own laws | s. 92(15) |
| Provinces | Prisons and reformatories (provincial jails) | s. 92(6) |

This is different from countries where each state writes its own criminal code. In
Canada a crime is defined the same way in every province.

### The main criminal laws

- The **Criminal Code** is the central criminal law. Canada's first Criminal Code was
  passed in 1892. It defines most offences (murder, assault, theft, fraud, impaired
  driving) and sets out criminal procedure.
- Other federal laws also create criminal offences, such as the *Controlled Drugs and
  Substances Act*.
- The *Youth Criminal Justice Act* sets special rules for people aged 12 to 17.

### Crimes vs. provincial offences

Provinces can't create crimes, but they can attach penalties to their own laws. A
speeding ticket, a liquor-licence violation or a fine under a provincial environmental
law are **provincial offences** (sometimes called regulatory or quasi-criminal
offences). They don't give you a criminal record under the Criminal Code. Cities can
also create bylaw offences, using power their province gives them.

If a province tries to pass what is really a criminal law, a court can strike it down as
outside provincial power. See [Federalism](/learn/federalism).

### Who does what, step by step

1. **Police investigate.** Most policing is provincial or municipal. Ontario and Quebec
   have their own provincial police forces. All other provinces, and all three
   territories, pay the **RCMP** (the federal police force) to act as their provincial
   police. In Newfoundland and Labrador, the RCMP shares that role with the province's
   own Royal Newfoundland Constabulary, which polices the St. John's area, Corner Brook
   and western Labrador. Many cities have their own police services.
2. **Prosecutors decide whether to lay or continue charges.** Prosecutors are called
   **Crown prosecutors** (or Crown counsel) because cases are brought in the name of the
   King, for example *R. v. Smith* ("R" stands for *Rex*, the King).
   - For Criminal Code offences in the provinces, prosecutors usually work for the
     **provincial Attorney General**.
   - The federal **[Public Prosecution Service of Canada](term:public-prosecution-service-of-canada)** handles drug offences and
     offences under other federal laws, and all criminal prosecutions in the three
     territories.
3. **A court hears the case.** Most criminal cases are heard in provincial court. The
   most serious ones are heard in a [superior court](term:superior-court), sometimes with a jury. See
   [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts).
4. **Sentences are carried out.** A total prison sentence of two years or more is served
   in a federal **[penitentiary](term:penitentiary)**, run by Correctional Service Canada. Shorter sentences
   are served in a provincial jail. This is often called the **two-year rule**.

### Three kinds of criminal offence

| Type | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **[Summary conviction](term:summary-conviction-offence)** | Less serious offences, with simpler procedure and lower maximum penalties | Causing a disturbance |
| **[Indictable](term:indictable-offence)** | More serious offences, with fuller procedure and higher maximum penalties | Murder, robbery |
| **[Hybrid](term:hybrid-offence)** | The prosecutor chooses whether to proceed summarily or by indictment | Many common offences, such as assault and impaired driving |

### The accused person's rights

Anyone charged with a crime is protected by the [Charter](doc:charter), especially
sections 7 to 14. These include the right to be **[presumed innocent](term:presumption-of-innocence)** until proven
guilty beyond a reasonable doubt, to be tried within a reasonable time, to a lawyer, and
to a jury trial for offences punishable by five years or more in prison. See
[Your Charter rights](/learn/charter/your-rights).
