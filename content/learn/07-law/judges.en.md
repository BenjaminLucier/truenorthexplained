## In short

Canadian judges are **appointed, never elected**. The federal government appoints judges
to the superior courts, the courts of appeal, the federal courts and the [Supreme Court](org:supreme-court).
Each province and territory appoints the judges of its own provincial or territorial
court. Once appointed, judges are protected by **judicial independence**: they can't be
removed for unpopular decisions, their pay is set at arm's length, and only the courts
decide which judge hears which case.

## The details

### Who appoints whom

| Court | Appointed by | In practice, chosen by |
|---|---|---|
| Supreme Court of Canada | Governor General (formally, the Governor in Council) | The Prime Minister |
| Chief justices and associate chief justices of superior courts and courts of appeal | Governor in Council | The Prime Minister |
| Other judges of superior courts, courts of appeal, the Federal Court, Federal Court of Appeal and Tax Court | Governor in Council | The federal Minister of Justice recommends |
| Provincial and territorial courts | The province or territory | Each province or territory has its own process, with no federal role |

"[Governor in Council](term:governor-in-council)" means the Governor General acting on the advice of the federal
cabinet. See [The Crown](org:crown).

### How federal candidates are screened

**Superior court and federal court judges.** Since 1988, lawyers and judges who want a
federal appointment apply through the **Office of the Commissioner for Federal Judicial
Affairs**. **[Judicial Advisory Committees](term:judicial-advisory-committees)** in each province and territory, made up of
people from the legal community and the public, review applications and assess
candidates. The Minister of Justice then makes recommendations to cabinet from among the
candidates the committees have assessed.

**Supreme Court judges.** Since 2016, an **Independent Advisory Board** reviews
applications and gives the Prime Minister a non-binding shortlist of three to five
candidates. The board has been asked to recommend only candidates who are **functionally
bilingual** in English and French. The Prime Minister makes the final choice.

> **As of 2026:** The advisory board process has been used for every Supreme Court
> appointment since 2016. It is a government policy, not a requirement in the
> Constitution or the *[Supreme Court Act](doc:supreme-court-act)*, so a future government could change it.

### Judicial independence: the three supports

Judges must decide cases only on the facts and the law, free from pressure from
government or anyone else. The Supreme Court has described three essential parts of
judicial independence (*Valente v. The Queen*, 1985):

1. **Security of tenure.** Judges hold office until retirement, not at the government's
   pleasure. They can't be removed for making decisions the government dislikes.
2. **Financial security.** Judges' pay is set by law. Governments can't cut it to punish
   judges, and since 1997 they must consider the recommendations of an independent
   **[compensation commission](term:compensation-commission)** before changing it (the Provincial Judges Reference).
3. **Administrative independence.** Courts control matters that affect how they decide
   cases, such as which judge hears which case. Only the chief justice assigns cases.

These protections exist for the public, not for judges' personal benefit. The point is
that people can trust a decision was made fairly.

### Retirement and removal

- **Federally appointed judges** must retire at **75**. This is in the Constitution for
  superior court judges (s. 99(2) of the Constitution Act, 1867) and in the *Supreme Court
  Act* for Supreme Court judges. Some provinces set an earlier retirement age, such as
  70, for their own judges.
- **Superior court judges** hold office "during good behaviour" (s. 99(1)). They can be
  removed only by the Governor General after a **[joint address](term:joint-address)** of the Senate and the
  House of Commons: both houses must vote to ask for the removal.
- Before that, the **Canadian Judicial Council** must investigate. The Council was created
  by Parliament in 1971 and is made up of the chief justices and associate chief justices
  of the federally appointed courts. It looks into complaints about federally appointed
  judges' conduct and can recommend removal to the Minister of Justice.

In practice, removal is extremely rare. Judges facing a removal recommendation have
generally resigned or retired before Parliament voted.

Provincial court judges are disciplined and removed under each province's own laws,
usually through a provincial judicial council.

### What judges don't do

- They don't run for office or belong to political parties while serving.
- They don't comment publicly on cases before them, or on political controversies.
- They don't give advice to the government, except through a formal
  [reference](/learn/law/supreme-court).
