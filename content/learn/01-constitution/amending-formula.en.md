## In short

Part V of the Constitution Act, 1982 (ss. 38–49) sets out **five amending procedures**.
Which one applies depends on what's being changed. Most significant changes need
Parliament plus seven provinces with half the population. Some, including anything
touching the monarchy, need Parliament plus **all ten** provinces. A 1996 federal law
adds a further layer of regional consent in practice.

## The details

### Who has to agree

An amendment under the multi-government procedures is made by a **proclamation** issued
by the Governor General, once the required **resolutions** have been passed. A
resolution is a formal vote in the Senate, the House of Commons or a provincial
legislature. A province's legislature, the Senate or the House can start the process
(s. 46(1)).

| Procedure | Section | Who must pass a resolution | What it covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **General ("7/50")** | s. 38 | Senate, House of Commons, and at least two-thirds of the provinces (7 of 10) with at least 50% of the population of all the provinces | Anything not covered elsewhere, plus the list in s. 42 |
| **Unanimity** | s. 41 | Senate, House of Commons, and all ten provincial legislatures | (a) the office of the King, the Governor General and the Lieutenant Governors; (b) each province's minimum number of MPs; (c) the use of English or French (subject to s. 43); (d) the composition of the Supreme Court; (e) the amending formula itself |
| **Some but not all provinces** | s. 43 | Senate, House of Commons, and each province affected | Provisions that apply only to some provinces, such as boundaries or language rules within a province |
| **Parliament alone** | s. 44 | Parliament (an ordinary Act) | The federal executive, Senate and House of Commons, except what ss. 41–42 protect |
| **A province alone** | s. 45 | That province's legislature (an ordinary Act) | The province's own constitution, except what s. 41 protects |

Section 42 lists matters that always need the 7/50 procedure, with no opting out. They
include proportionate representation in the Commons, the Senate's powers and how
senators are selected, the number of senators per province, the Supreme Court (other
than its composition), extending provinces into the territories, and creating new
provinces.

### The fine print that makes 7/50 harder

- **Population.** Because Ontario and Quebec together hold well over half the
  population, the 7/50 rule can't be met without at least one of them. <!-- check: confirm against current census figures -->
- **Time limits.** A 7/50 amendment can't be proclaimed within one year of the first
  resolution unless every province has already voted yes or no, and it can't be
  proclaimed after three years (s. 39).
- **Opting out.** If an amendment takes power away from the provinces, a province can
  pass a resolution of dissent and the amendment won't apply to it (s. 38(3)). If the
  power transferred relates to education or other cultural matters, Ottawa must pay
  that province reasonable compensation (s. 40).
- **Changing your mind.** A province can withdraw its yes vote any time before the
  proclamation (s. 46(2)).
- **The Senate can only delay.** For the multi-government procedures, if the Senate
  hasn't passed the resolution within 180 days of the House doing so, the House can
  pass it again and go ahead without the Senate (s. 47).

### The extra layer: the 1996 "regional veto" law

After the 1995 Quebec referendum, Parliament passed *An Act respecting constitutional
amendments*. It says no federal minister may propose a 7/50 amendment unless it has
first been approved by a majority of provinces that includes:

1. Ontario
2. Quebec
3. British Columbia
4. Two or more Atlantic provinces with at least 50% of the Atlantic population
5. Two or more Prairie provinces with at least 50% of the Prairie population

This isn't part of the Constitution. It's an ordinary federal statute that limits what
federal ministers will do, and Parliament could repeal it. But while it stands, it
makes the general procedure much harder to use.

### Political add-ons

Beyond the legal rules, some provinces (including British Columbia and Alberta) have
laws requiring a referendum before their legislature votes on certain constitutional
amendments. <!-- check: current BC Constitutional Amendment Approval Act and Alberta Constitutional Referendum Act requirements --> And since the 1992 Charlottetown referendum, many people
expect a national referendum for any major change, although none is legally required.

### What the courts have said

The Supreme Court has made clear that you can't get around these procedures by passing
an ordinary law:

- **Reference re Senate Reform (2014):** Parliament alone can't create elections for
  senators or set term limits. Those need 7/50. Abolishing the Senate needs unanimity.
- **Reference re Supreme Court Act, ss. 5 and 6 (2014):** the rule that at least three
  of the nine judges come from Quebec is part of the Court's "composition", so it can only be
  changed with unanimity.

### Has it ever been used?

Yes, but mostly for narrow changes:

| Procedure | Examples |
|---|---|
| General (7/50) | 1983: strengthening the protection of Aboriginal and treaty rights in s. 35 <!-- check: only use of s. 38 to date --> |
| Bilateral (s. 43) | 1993: equality of New Brunswick's English and French communities (s. 16.1 of the Charter). 1997: removing denominational school guarantees in Quebec (s. 93A). 2001: renaming Newfoundland as Newfoundland and Labrador. |
| Parliament alone (s. 44) | Changes to the formula for distributing House of Commons seats |
| A province alone (s. 45) | 2022: Quebec's additions to the 1867 Act (ss. 90Q.1–90Q.2). 2023: Saskatchewan's (s. 90S.1). |

The unanimity procedure has never been successfully used. Its one serious attempt, the
Meech Lake Accord, failed. See [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982).

### Why make it so hard?

The Constitution protects the deals that hold a federation together: the provinces'
powers, Quebec's language and legal traditions, minority rights. A high bar means no
single government, or a temporary majority, can rewrite those deals. The cost is that
even widely discussed changes, such as Senate reform or ending the monarchy (see
[Could Canada drop the monarchy?](/learn/crown/republic-question)), are very hard to
achieve. People disagree about whether that trade-off is right.
