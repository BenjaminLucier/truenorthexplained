## In short

The federal government sends billions of dollars a year to the provinces and
[territories](term:territory) through four **major transfers**. The **Canada Health Transfer** and
**Canada Social Transfer** go to everyone on an equal per-person basis. **Equalization**
goes only to provinces whose ability to raise revenue is below the national standard.
**Territorial Formula Financing** does a similar job for the three territories.

## The details

### Why transfers exist

Provinces are responsible for some of the most expensive public services: health care,
education and social assistance. The federal government has broad taxing powers but
fewer big spending responsibilities of its own. Transfers move money from where it's
raised to where it's spent.

There's also a second gap, between provinces. Some have more taxable income, business
activity or natural resources than others, so the same tax rate brings in very
different amounts. Equalization is aimed at that gap.

### The four major transfers

| Transfer | Who gets it | What it's for | How it's shared | Strings attached? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Canada Health Transfer (CHT)** | All provinces and territories | Health care | Equal amount per person | Yes: provinces must follow the *Canada Health Act* or risk deductions |
| **Canada Social Transfer (CST)** | All provinces and territories | Post-secondary education, social assistance and social services, early childhood development and child care | Equal amount per person | Few. The main one is that provinces can't impose a minimum residency period for social assistance |
| **Equalization** | Only provinces below the national standard | Anything; it's unconditional | Formula based on "fiscal capacity" | No |
| **Territorial Formula Financing (TFF)** | The three territories | Anything; it's unconditional | A separate formula for each territory | No |

The CHT and CST were created in 2004, when an earlier combined transfer (the Canada
Health and Social Transfer) was split in two.

### The Canada Health Transfer and the Canada Health Act

The CHT is the largest federal transfer. To get their full payment, provinces and
territories must run their health insurance plans according to the **Canada Health
Act**, which sets five criteria: **public administration**, **comprehensiveness**,
**universality**, **portability** and **accessibility**. It also bans extra-billing by
doctors and user charges for insured services. Ottawa has deducted money from provinces
that allowed these charges.

> **As of 2026:** Under a 2023 agreement, the CHT is guaranteed to grow by at least 5%
> a year from 2023–24 to 2027–28. Otherwise it grows with the economy (a three-year
> average of nominal GDP), with a floor of 3% a year. The CST grows by 3% a year.

### Equalization

The [Constitution Act, 1982](doc:constitution-act-1982) commits the federal government to equalization. Section
36(2) says Parliament and the government of Canada are committed to the principle of
making equalization payments so that provinces can provide "reasonably comparable
levels of public services at reasonably comparable levels of taxation". The program
itself is older: it began in **[1957](event:equalization-begins-1957)**.

How it works, simplified:

1. For each province, the federal [Department of Finance](term:department-of-finance) calculates its **fiscal
   capacity**: how much revenue it *could* raise if it applied national average tax
   rates. This is a measure of potential, not of what it actually collects.
2. Five revenue sources are measured: personal income tax, business income tax,
   consumption taxes, property taxes and natural resource revenues.
3. Provinces whose fiscal capacity per person is below the standard receive payments.
   Provinces above it receive nothing.
4. The total size of the program grows with the economy, following a three-year moving
   average of national nominal GDP growth.

Every province has received equalization at some point since 1957.

> [!MYTH]
> **"Some provinces pay equalization to others."** Equalization is paid out of the
> federal government's **general revenues**, mostly federal taxes paid by people and
> businesses across the country, including in provinces that receive payments.
> Provinces don't pay into it. Saying a province "pays equalization" really means that
> its residents pay federal taxes, some of which fund the program.

**Territories don't get equalization.** They have their own program, TFF.

> **As of 2026:** The equalization program is set by federal law in five-year periods.
> The next renewal must happen before March 31, 2029.

### Territorial Formula Financing

Delivering services in the North costs much more than in the south, and the territories
have small tax bases. TFF is an annual unconditional grant to each territorial
government. Each territory has its own **gap-filling** formula: roughly, its estimated
spending needs minus its capacity to raise revenue. Only part of a territory's own
revenue capacity is counted, so territories still have a reason to grow their own
economies. TFF has existed since 1985.

### Other money flows

The four major transfers aren't the whole story. Ottawa also funds targeted programs
(for example child care agreements, infrastructure, and housing), often negotiated one
province at a time. And some transfers have historically taken the form of **[tax
points](term:tax-points)**: Ottawa lowered its own tax rates so provinces could raise theirs by the same
amount.

> **People disagree about this.** Equalization is one of the most argued-over parts of
> federalism. Some argue the formula is unfair to resource-rich provinces, or that it
> discourages economic development; others argue it's essential to the idea that every
> Canadian should get comparable services wherever they live. In 2021, Alberta held a
> [provincial referendum](event:alberta-equalization-referendum-2021) in which a majority voted to remove the
> equalization commitment from the Constitution. A referendum on its own doesn't amend
> the Constitution.
