## In short

The [Constitution Act, 1867](doc:constitution-act-1867) gives Parliament a list of subjects in **section 91** and
the provinces a list in **section 92**. A few other sections add natural resources,
education, pensions, agriculture and immigration. Anything left over goes to Parliament
under its **"peace, order and good government"** power. When valid federal and
provincial laws truly conflict, the federal law wins.

### How to read the lists

Sections 91 and 92 list **"classes of subjects"**, also called **heads of power**.
Each head is a short phrase, like "Banking" or "Hospitals". Courts decide which head a
law falls under by asking what the law is mainly about, called its **"pith and
substance"**. A law that's mainly about a valid subject can still affect other subjects
along the way.

The wording is from 1867, so some of it is old-fashioned (for example "Indians" in
[s. 91(24)](doc:constitution-act-1867/s91-24), or "Asylums" in s. 92(7)). Courts read the lists as a "[living tree](term:living-tree)" that
applies to new things such as broadcasting and aviation.

| 92(12) | Solemnization of marriage (the ceremony and who can perform it) |
| 92(13) | Property and civil rights in the province |
| 92(14) | Administration of justice: setting up and running provincial courts, and civil procedure |
| 92(15) | Fines, penalties and imprisonment to enforce provincial laws |
| 92(16) | Generally, all matters of a merely local or private nature in the province |

**Property and civil rights** (s. 92(13)) is the big one. "Civil rights" here doesn't
mean civil liberties. It means private legal relationships: contracts, property,
families, most employment and most business regulation. That's why most labour law,
consumer protection and securities regulation are provincial.

### Other sections that add powers

- **s. 92A (added 1982): natural resources.** Provinces have exclusive power over
  exploring for, developing, conserving and managing **non-renewable natural
  resources** (oil, gas, minerals) and **forestry resources**, and over **electricity**
  generation. They may also regulate exports of these to other provinces, but a federal
  law prevails if the two conflict. They can tax these resources by any mode, including
  indirect taxes, as long as the tax doesn't discriminate against other provinces.
- **s. 93: education.** Provinces make laws about education, subject to protections for
  certain denominational (religious) school rights that existed when a province joined.
- **s. 94A: pensions.** Parliament may make laws about old age pensions and related
  benefits (including survivors' and disability benefits), but no federal law affects
  a provincial law on the same subject. This is the one case where **provincial** law
  wins a conflict. It's why Quebec can run the [Quebec Pension Plan](term:quebec-pension-plan) alongside the Canada
  Pension Plan. Added in 1951 and broadened in 1964.
- **s. 95: agriculture and immigration.** Both Parliament and the provinces can make
  laws on these. A provincial law applies only as long as it isn't "repugnant to"
  (inconsistent with) a federal law.

These are called **concurrent** powers: both orders can legislate on the same subject.

### The federal catch-all: peace, order and good government

Section 91 opens by giving Parliament power to make laws for the **"Peace, Order, and
good Government of Canada"** (often shortened to **POGG**) on all matters not given
exclusively to the provinces. This is the **residual power**: whatever isn't listed
goes to Ottawa. (In the United States it's the opposite: leftover powers go to the
states.)

Courts have recognized three ways POGG applies:

1. **Gaps:** subjects the drafters plainly left out, such as incorporating companies
   with federal (not provincial) objects.
2. **National concern:** a distinct new matter that the provinces can't deal with on
   their own. In 2021 the [Supreme Court](org:supreme-court) used this to uphold the federal law setting a
   minimum national price on greenhouse gas emissions, while stressing that the branch
   applies only rarely.
3. **Emergency:** temporary federal laws to deal with a genuine national crisis, such as
   war. The Supreme Court upheld federal wage and price controls on this basis in 1976.

### When laws collide

Several court-made doctrines handle overlap:

- **Double aspect.** Some subjects have both a federal and a provincial side, so both
  orders can legislate. Impaired driving is a crime (federal) and also a reason to
  suspend a driver's licence (provincial highway rules).
- **Paramountcy.** If a valid federal law and a valid provincial law truly conflict,
  because you can't obey both or the provincial law defeats the purpose of the federal
  one, the **federal law prevails**. The provincial law isn't struck down; it just
  doesn't apply to the extent of the conflict. Section 94A (pensions) is the exception
  where the provincial law wins.
- **Cooperative federalism.** Modern courts prefer to let both laws operate where they
  can, rather than looking for conflicts.

### Two tricky pairs

| Federal | Provincial |
|---|---|
| Marriage and divorce (s. 91(26)): who can marry, and divorce | Solemnization of marriage (s. 92(12)): the ceremony, licences, who can officiate |
| Criminal law and procedure ([s. 91(27)](doc:constitution-act-1867/s91-27)), penitentiaries (s. 91(28)) | Running the courts ([s. 92(14)](doc:constitution-act-1867/s92-14)), provincial prisons (s. 92(6)) |

The second pair is why the *[Criminal Code](term:criminal-code)* is one federal law, but most criminal cases
are prosecuted by provincial [Crown attorneys](term:crown-prosecutor) in provincially run courts. In practice,
people sentenced to two years or more go to federal penitentiaries and shorter sentences
are served in provincial jails. See [criminal law](/learn/law/criminal-law).
