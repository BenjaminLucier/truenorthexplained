## In short

**Reserve powers** are the Crown's emergency brakes: decisions the Governor General
(or a Lieutenant Governor) can make on their own judgment instead of on a minister's
advice. They exist to protect [responsible government](term:responsible-government), not to let the Crown govern. They
are rarely used, and every real use has been controversial. The two federal cases most
often studied are the **[King–Byng affair](event:king-byng-affair)** of 1926 and the **[2008 prorogation](event:prorogation-2008)**.

## The details

### What counts as a reserve power

Nothing in the written Constitution lists them. They come from convention and
precedent, so experts disagree about the edges. The ones most commonly cited:

| Power | When it might be used |
|---|---|
| **Appointing a Prime Minister** | When no one clearly commands the confidence of the House, e.g. after an inconclusive election or a sudden death in office |
| **Dismissing a Prime Minister** | Only if a government refused to resign after clearly losing confidence, or acted flagrantly unconstitutionally. Never done federally. |
| **Refusing a [dissolution](term:dissolution)** | When a Prime Minister asks for an election to avoid a confidence vote, or soon after an election, and another leader could form a government |
| **Refusing a prorogation** | When the request appears designed to avoid a confidence vote. Very contested. |
| **Refusing [royal assent](term:royal-assent)** | Theoretically possible. Not done federally, and widely considered obsolete. |

The Governor General's office describes these powers as making the Governor General "a
democratic safeguard". The guiding idea: the Crown acts on advice, *unless* the advice
would stop the House of Commons from deciding who governs.

### 1926: the King–Byng affair

**Background.** In the 1925 election, the Conservatives under [Arthur Meighen](person:arthur-meighen) won the
most seats, but Liberal Prime Minister [](person:william-lyon-mackenzie-king) stayed in office
with the support of smaller parties. This is allowed: the sitting Prime Minister gets
the first chance to test the House's confidence.

**The crisis.** By June 1926, a scandal in the customs department had put King's
government at risk of losing a vote in the House. On **June 26, 1926**, King asked Governor General [Lord Byng](person:julian-byng)
to dissolve Parliament and call an election. Byng refused. He reasoned that the
election had been recent and that Meighen should get a chance to govern. (The
Dictionary of Canadian Biography dates King's formal request June 28.)

**What happened next.**

1. King resigned on June 28.
2. Byng invited Meighen to form a government, and Meighen became Prime Minister on
   June 29.
3. On July 1, after only three days facing the House, Meighen's government lost a
   confidence vote by a single vote. (The Dictionary of Canadian Biography puts the
   vote at about 2 a.m. on July 2.)
4. Meighen asked for a dissolution, and Byng granted it. Parliament was dissolved on
   July 2.
5. King campaigned partly on the Governor General's conduct and won the election of
   September 14, 1926.

**Why it matters.** Byng's refusal was arguably within his powers, but treating the
two leaders differently looked unfair, and voters rewarded King. The episode is often
linked to the **[1926 Imperial Conference](event:imperial-conference-1926)** later that year, which established that a Governor General
represents the monarch personally, not the British government. House of Commons
Procedure and Practice gives 1926 as its only example of a Governor General refusing a
federal Prime Minister's request to dissolve Parliament.

### 2008: the prorogation

**Background.** After the October 2008 election, [Stephen Harper](person:stephen-harper)'s Conservatives led a
[minority government](term:minority-government). In late November, the government's fiscal update angered the
opposition. The Liberals and NDP agreed to form a [coalition](term:coalition) government, with a
supporting agreement from the Bloc Québécois, and planned to defeat the government in
a confidence vote in early December.

**The request.** On **December 4, 2008**, before the vote could happen, Harper asked
Governor General [](person:michaelle-jean) to **[prorogue](term:prorogation)** Parliament: to end the session, which
also cancels all pending votes. She agreed, but only after several hours.
Parliament didn't return until **January 26, 2009**.

**What happened next.** The coalition fell apart over the break. The government
presented a budget in January 2009, and the Liberals supported it with conditions, so
the government survived.

**Why it matters.** The Prime Minister had asked for a prorogation to avoid an imminent
confidence vote. A House of Commons committee has pointed to one earlier case: in 1873,
John A. Macdonald was granted a prorogation while losing support in the House during the
Pacific Scandal. People still disagree about whether the Governor General was right:

- **For granting it:** Governors General should follow the advice of a Prime Minister
  who hasn't yet lost a confidence vote. Prorogation was a pause, not a cancellation:
  the House would get its vote eventually.
- **Against granting it:** The point of the reserve powers is to protect the House's
  right to decide who governs. Proroguing to avoid a vote undercut exactly that.

Later prorogations have also drawn criticism. A court challenge to a federal
prorogation in early 2025 was dismissed by the [Federal Court](org:federal-court) in March 2025, according to
Wikipedia's article on prorogation in Canada (*MacKinnon v. Canada*, 2025 FC 422).

### Provincial cases

[Lieutenant Governors](org:lieutenant-governor) have used reserve powers more often, mostly long ago:

- **1878, Quebec:** Lieutenant Governor Luc Letellier de Saint-Just dismissed Premier
  Charles Boucher de Boucherville's government.
- **1961, Saskatchewan:** Lieutenant Governor Frank Bastedo reserved a provincial bill
  for the federal government's decision. Ottawa approved it by order in council. This is [the last
  use of the reservation power](event:last-reservation-1961).

### The bigger picture

Reserve powers are like a fire extinguisher: you hope never to use one, and using it
always leaves a mess. The Governor General's real influence comes from the possibility
that they *could* say no, which encourages governments to keep their requests within
accepted conventions. See also [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)
and [Majority, minority, confidence votes](/learn/parliament/confidence).
