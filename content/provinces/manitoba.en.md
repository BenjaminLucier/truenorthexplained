## How it's governed

Manitoba's legislature is the **Legislative Assembly**, which has a single chamber.
Manitoba started out with an appointed upper house too, the **[Legislative Council](term:legislative-council)**,
but abolished it in 1876, only six years after joining Canada. Voters elect one **[MLA](term:mla)**
(Member of the Legislative Assembly) for each electoral division.

The party that can keep the **confidence** (majority support) of the Assembly forms
the government, and its leader becomes **premier**. See
[Confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence).

The **Lieutenant Governor** represents the King. They give **royal assent** to bills
and carry out other formal duties on the advice of the elected government. See
[The Crown](/learn/crown).

## What makes it different

### A province negotiated by the Métis

In 1869, Canada arranged to take over **[Rupert's Land](event:ruperts-land-1870)**, a huge territory controlled
until then by the Hudson's Bay Company. Nobody asked the people already living at Red
River (around today's Winnipeg), most of whom were **[Métis](term:metis)**: a distinct people of
mixed First Nations and European ancestry, with their own culture and language.

The Métis, led by **Louis Riel**, formed a **provisional government** (a temporary
government set up until a permanent one exists) and sent delegates to negotiate with
Ottawa. This is known as the **Red River Resistance** (1869–70). The result was the
**Manitoba Act, 1870**, which made Manitoba a province on July 15, 1870. Riel was later
executed for treason in 1885, after leading the North-West Resistance in what is now
Saskatchewan. His role in history has been debated ever since.

Manitoba now formally honours him. In 2023 the legislature passed **The Louis Riel
Act**, which gives him the honorary title of "First Premier of Manitoba". He never held
that office.

### Rights written into the founding law

The Manitoba Act is part of Canada's Constitution, and two of its sections still
matter:

- **Section 23** says English and French may be used in the legislature and the
  courts, and that Manitoba's laws must be printed and published in both languages.
  In 1890, Manitoba passed a law making English its only official language. In 1985,
  the Supreme Court of Canada ruled in the *Reference re Manitoba Language Rights* that
  every law passed only in English was invalid. To avoid leaving the province with no
  valid laws at all, it gave Manitoba time to translate and re-enact them.
- **Section 31** set aside 1.4 million acres of land for the children of Métis
  families. Distributing it was badly delayed and mishandled. In 2013, in *Manitoba
  Metis Federation v. Canada*, the Supreme Court ruled that Canada had not carried out
  this promise in the honourable way the Constitution requires.

### A "postage stamp" province that grew

The original province was a small square around Red River, nicknamed the "postage
stamp province". Its borders were pushed out in 1881 and again in 1912 to reach its
current size. Like Alberta and Saskatchewan, Manitoba didn't control its public land
and natural resources until the **[Natural Resources Transfer Agreements](term:natural-resources-transfer-agreements)** of 1930.

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Court of Appeal of Manitoba** | The province's highest court. Hears appeals. |
| **Court of King's Bench of Manitoba** | The **superior court**: serious criminal trials, larger civil cases and family law (through its Family Division). Its judges are appointed by the federal government. |
| **Provincial Court of Manitoba** | Handles most criminal cases, plus youth and some family matters. Its judges are appointed by the province. |

## Local government

Most Manitoba municipalities are governed by **The Municipal Act**. Winnipeg has its own
law, **The City of Winnipeg Charter**. In 1972, Winnipeg and its surrounding
municipalities were merged into a single city, often called **[Unicity](term:unicity)**.

Not every part of Manitoba is inside a municipality. Many small northern communities
are set up under a separate law, The Northern Affairs Act. <!-- check: Northern Affairs Act still the governing law for these communities --> See
[Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities).
