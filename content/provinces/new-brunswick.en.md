## How it's governed

New Brunswick's legislature is the **Legislative Assembly**, which has a single
chamber. It once had an appointed upper house too, the **[Legislative Council](term:legislative-council)**, which
was abolished in 1892. Voters elect one **[MLA](term:mla)** (Member of the Legislative Assembly)
for each electoral district.

The party that can keep the **confidence** (majority support) of the Assembly forms the
government, and its leader becomes **premier**. See
[Confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence).

The **Lieutenant Governor** represents the King. They give **royal assent** to bills
and carry out other formal duties on the advice of the elected government. See
[The Crown](/learn/crown).

## What makes it different

### Canada's only officially bilingual province

New Brunswick is the only province where English and French have **equal status** in
the Constitution itself. A large share of the population, about 3 in 10 people, speaks
French as a first language, most of them **[Acadians](term:acadians)**: descendants of the French
settlers of the Maritime region.

The **[Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms](doc:charter)** gives New Brunswick its own
language guarantees, in sections that apply to no other province:

| Charter section | What it guarantees in New Brunswick |
|---|---|
| [s. 16(2)](doc:charter/s16) | English and French are the official languages, with equal status, rights and privileges in the legislature and government |
| [s. 16.1](doc:charter/s16-1) | The English and French **linguistic communities** have equal status, including the right to their own educational and cultural institutions (added in 1993) |
| s. 17(2) | Either language may be used in the legislature's debates |
| s. 18(2) | Laws and records of the legislature are printed in both languages, and both versions are equally authoritative |
| s. 19(2) | Either language may be used in New Brunswick courts |
| s. 20(2) | People can get services from provincial government offices in either language |

These protections are part of the Constitution, so the province couldn't remove them
with an ordinary law. New Brunswick also has its own **Official Languages Act** (the
first one was passed in 1969) and a **Commissioner of Official Languages for New
Brunswick**, who handles complaints.

### Services organized by language

Because of the equal-communities principle, some public services are organized by
language. New Brunswick has separate English-language and French-language school
systems, and two regional health authorities, one of which operates mainly in French.

See [Your rights](/learn/charter/your-rights) for the Charter in general.

### Origins

New Brunswick was split off from Nova Scotia as a separate British colony in 1784,
largely to govern the many **Loyalists** (colonists who had stayed loyal to Britain
during the American Revolution) who had settled there. In 1867 it became one of the
four founding provinces of Canada.

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Court of Appeal of New Brunswick** | The province's highest court. Hears appeals. |
| **Court of King's Bench of New Brunswick** | The **superior court**, with a Trial Division (serious criminal trials, larger civil cases) and a Family Division. Its judges are appointed by the federal government. |
| **Provincial Court of New Brunswick** | Handles most criminal cases, including youth cases. Its judges are appointed by the province. |

As required by s. 19(2) of the Charter, anyone can use English or French in these
courts. Above them is the [Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court). See
[Courts](/learn/law/courts).

## Local government

New Brunswick's local governments are set up under the **Local Governance Act**. In
2023, a major reform took effect. It merged and restructured hundreds of small local
entities, including many unincorporated **[local service districts](term:local-service-district)**, into a much
smaller number of local governments and **rural districts**.

The reform also gave the province's 12 **regional service commissions** a bigger role
in regional planning, economic development and other shared services.

> **As of 2026:** New Brunswick has 77 local governments and 12 rural districts, grouped
> into 12 regional service commissions.

See [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities).
