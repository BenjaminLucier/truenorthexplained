## How it's governed

The Northwest Territories (NWT) is a **territory**, not a province. That difference
is the key to understanding it.

- **Provinces** get their powers straight from the Constitution. **Territories** get
  theirs from an ordinary federal law, here the **Northwest Territories Act**. In
  principle, Parliament could change those powers. In practice, it has steadily
  handed more to the territory over the decades. See
  [Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories).
- **The Commissioner** plays the role a Lieutenant Governor plays in a province:
  giving assent to laws, opening sessions of the Assembly, and appointing the
  executive. But unlike a Lieutenant Governor, the Commissioner is not the King's
  personal representative: they are appointed by the **federal cabinet**. Today the role is
  largely ceremonial, and the Commissioner acts on the advice of the territory's
  elected government.
- **The Legislature** is made up of the Commissioner and the Legislative Assembly,
  whose 19 **MLAs** (members of the Legislative Assembly) pass the territory's laws.
- **The Premier** leads the territorial government, chosen in a very unusual way
  (below).

## What makes it different

### Consensus government: no parties

NWT elections have **no political parties**. Every candidate runs as an independent.
After the election, the 19 MLAs choose the government themselves:

1. The MLAs meet as a caucus and set shared priorities.
2. They elect a **Speaker** by secret ballot.
3. Candidates for **Premier** give speeches and take questions, then MLAs vote by
   secret ballot, round after round if needed, until someone wins a majority.
4. MLAs elect **six cabinet ministers** the same way. The Premier then decides which
   departments each one runs.
5. The MLAs left outside cabinet (other than the Speaker) are the **Regular Members**. They act as an
   unofficial opposition, questioning and checking the government.

Because cabinet (7 with the Premier) is outnumbered by Regular Members, the
government can't simply push its plans through. It has to win support issue by issue.
This model, called **consensus government**, draws on Indigenous traditions of
decision-making by agreement. Nunavut uses a similar system (see
[Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)).

People disagree about it. Supporters say it encourages co-operation and keeps
government close to communities. Critics say it makes it hard for voters to choose a
direction for the territory or hold anyone accountable at election time.

Compare this with [how a Prime Minister gets the job](/learn/elections), where
parties decide almost everything.

### Eleven official languages

The NWT recognizes **11 official languages** under its Official Languages Act:
Dene Kǝdǝ́ (North Slavey), Dëne Sųłıné (Chipewyan), Dene Zhatıé (South Slavey),
Dinjii Zhuʼ Ginjik (Gwich'in), English, French, Inuinnaqtun, Inuktitut,
Inuvialuktun, nēhiyawēwin (Cree) and Tłı̨chǫ. No other jurisdiction in Canada has
as many.

### Shrinking borders

When Canada acquired the region in 1870, the "North-West Territories" covered most of
what is now western and northern Canada. Over time it was carved up: Manitoba
(1870), Yukon (1898), Alberta and Saskatchewan (1905), large northern additions to
Manitoba, Ontario and Quebec (1912), and finally **Nunavut (1999)**, which took the
eastern half of the territory. Yellowknife has been the capital since 1967.

### Devolution: control of land and resources

For most of its history, the federal government managed the NWT's public lands,
water and minerals. On **April 1, 2014**, under a **devolution** agreement between
Ottawa, the territorial government and several Indigenous governments, those
responsibilities passed to the NWT. A new Northwest Territories Act came into force
the same day. The NWT now collects a share of resource revenues and makes most
decisions about lands and resources, much like a province. Legally, what passed to
the territory was the *administration and control* of the land, not the title to it.

### Land claims and Indigenous self-government

About half of NWT residents are Indigenous. Large parts of the territory are covered
by modern treaties (land claim agreements), including the **Inuvialuit Final
Agreement** (1984) and the **Tłı̨chǫ Agreement** (2003), which also set up the Tłı̨chǫ
Government. Negotiations continue in other regions. See
[Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government).

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Court of Appeal for the Northwest Territories** | Hears appeals. Its judges are drawn from the NWT and from other courts, including those of Alberta, Saskatchewan, Yukon and Nunavut. <!-- check: current composition of the NWT Court of Appeal --> |
| **Supreme Court of the Northwest Territories** | The **superior court**: serious criminal trials, larger civil cases, divorce. Despite the name, it is not the highest court. |
| **Territorial Court of the Northwest Territories** | Hears most criminal cases, youth cases and some civil claims. It also runs specialized courts such as a Wellness Court. |
| **Justices of the peace** | Handle minor offences and bail hearings, especially in small communities. |

Judges often travel to small communities on **circuit**, holding court where people
live. See [the court structure](/learn/law/courts).

## Local government

The NWT has 33 communities, most of them small. Local governments include cities,
towns and villages, as well as hamlets, charter communities and settlements, and in
some places Indigenous governments deliver local services. Yellowknife is the only
city.
