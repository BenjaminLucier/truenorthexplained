## How it's governed

Nova Scotia's legislature has a single elected chamber, the **House of Assembly**.
Its members are called **MLAs** (Members of the Legislative Assembly). The province
had an appointed upper house too, the **Legislative Council**, until it was abolished
in 1928.

The party that can keep the **confidence** (majority support) of the House forms the
government, and its leader becomes **premier**. See
[Confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence).

The **Lieutenant Governor** represents the King. They give **royal assent** to bills
and carry out other formal duties on the advice of the elected government. See
[The Crown](/learn/crown).

## What makes it different

### The oldest elected assembly in Canada

Nova Scotia's House of Assembly first met in Halifax on **October 2, 1758**, making it
the oldest elected legislature in what is now Canada. It was far from democratic by
today's standards: only Protestant men who owned land could vote.

The House has met since 1819 in **Province House** in Halifax, which is often described
as the longest-serving legislative building in Canada.

### The first responsible government

For most of its early history, the colony's governor and his appointed council made
the real decisions, whatever the elected assembly wanted. Reformers pushed for
**responsible government**: the idea that the government must have the support of the
elected assembly, and must resign if it loses it. This is the
[confidence convention](/learn/parliament/confidence) that every Canadian government
still follows today.

Reformers won an election on that platform, and in **February 1848** Nova Scotia
became the first colony in the British Empire, outside the United Kingdom itself, to
have responsible government. The Province of Canada followed a few weeks later. See
[The unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions).

### A reluctant founding province

Nova Scotia was one of the four provinces that founded Canada in 1867, but the decision
was unpopular at home. In the first elections after Confederation, candidates opposed
to it won most of the province's seats, and some leaders tried to get Britain to let
Nova Scotia leave. They didn't succeed.

### Protected seats for Acadian and African Nova Scotian communities

When electoral boundaries were redrawn for the 2021 election, Nova Scotia restored four
districts drawn to help minority communities elect a representative: three with large
**Acadian** (French-speaking) populations, Argyle, Clare and Richmond, and **Preston**,
home to a historic **African Nova Scotian** community. They had been eliminated in an
earlier redistribution, which led to a court challenge.

### Peace and Friendship Treaties

In the 1700s, the British Crown signed **Peace and Friendship Treaties** with the
Mi'kmaq and other peoples of the region. Unlike most later treaties, they didn't
involve giving up land. In *R. v. Marshall* (1999), the Supreme Court of Canada ruled
that these treaties protect a Mi'kmaq right to fish and trade to earn a moderate
livelihood. See [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties).

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Nova Scotia Court of Appeal** | The province's highest court. Hears appeals. |
| **Supreme Court of Nova Scotia** | The **superior trial court**, which includes a Family Division. Despite the name, it isn't the province's highest court. Its judges are appointed by the federal government. |
| **Provincial Court of Nova Scotia** | Handles most criminal cases, including youth cases. Its judges are appointed by the province. |

Nova Scotia also has specialized courts, such as Small Claims Court and Probate Court.
Above them all is the [Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court). See
[Courts](/learn/law/courts).

## Local government

Most municipalities are governed by the **Municipal Government Act**. Halifax has its
own law, the **Halifax Regional Municipality Charter**.

In the 1990s, Nova Scotia merged a number of municipalities into large **regional
municipalities**. The two biggest are the **Cape Breton Regional Municipality** (1995)
and the **Halifax Regional Municipality** (1996), each covering a mix of cities, towns
and rural areas. See [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities).
