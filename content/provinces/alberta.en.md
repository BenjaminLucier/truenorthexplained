## How it's governed

Alberta's legislature has a single chamber, the **Legislative Assembly**, which has
been the case since the province was created. Voters in each electoral division elect
one **MLA** (Member of the Legislative Assembly).

The party that can keep the **confidence** (the majority support) of the Assembly forms
the government. Its leader becomes **premier**, the head of government, and chooses
cabinet ministers from among the MLAs. This works the same way as
[the federal system](/learn/parliament), just at the provincial level.

The **Lieutenant Governor** represents the King in Alberta. They give **royal assent**
(the final sign-off) to bills, swear in the premier and cabinet, and open and dissolve
the legislature. As with the Governor General federally, they act on the advice of the
elected government. See [The Crown](/learn/crown).

## What makes it different

### A province that had to wait for its resources

Alberta was created by an Act of the federal Parliament, the **Alberta Act**, in 1905.
The original provinces kept ownership of their public land and natural resources when
they joined Canada. Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba didn't. Ottawa kept control of
their Crown land and resources until the **Natural Resources Transfer Agreements** of
**1930**, which the Constitution Act, 1930 made part of the Constitution.

That history matters because Alberta's economy is built heavily on oil and natural
gas. Today the province owns most of the minerals under its land, collects
**royalties** (payments companies make for extracting them), and regulates how they're
developed.

### Section 92A: resources in the Constitution

In 1982, a new **section 92A** was added to the Constitution Act, 1867. It confirms
that provinces have exclusive power over:

- exploring for, developing, conserving and managing **non-renewable natural
  resources** (such as oil, gas and minerals) and forestry resources;
- generating electricity;
- taxing those resources.

It also lets provinces make laws about exporting those resources to other provinces,
as long as they don't discriminate in prices or supply. Federal laws still win if the
two conflict on interprovincial exports. Section 92A was added partly in response to
disputes between Ottawa and resource-producing provinces in the 1970s and early 1980s.
Arguments over who controls energy, pipelines and environmental rules continue, and
people disagree about where the line should sit. See
[Who does what](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers).

### Recall and citizen initiatives

Alberta and British Columbia are the only provinces where voters can recall their member
of the legislature, and both also have full citizen-initiative laws.
<!-- check: confirm no other province currently has a recall law -->
Voters in Alberta can start two kinds of petition:

- **Recall:** if enough voters in an electoral division sign a petition, a vote can be
  held on whether to remove their MLA before the next election.
- **Citizen initiative:** if enough voters across the province sign, a proposed law or
  policy question can be sent to the legislature or to a **referendum** (a public vote
  on a question).

Both come from laws passed in 2021, the **Recall Act** and the **Citizen Initiative
Act**, and are run by Elections Alberta.

> **As of 2026:** Changes that took effect on July 4, 2025 lowered the number of
> signatures needed for a citizen initiative petition. Check Elections Alberta for the
> current rules.

### Métis Settlements

Alberta is the only province with a legislated Métis land base. Under the **Metis
Settlements Act** (1990), eight Métis Settlements hold land collectively and have
their own elected councils. <!-- check: confirm "only province" wording and the 1990 date against Alberta's Metis Settlements Act -->

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Court of Appeal of Alberta** | The province's highest court. Hears appeals from the other courts and from some tribunals. |
| **Court of King's Bench of Alberta** | The **superior court**: the main trial court for serious criminal cases and larger civil cases, and it hears some appeals from the Court of Justice. Its judges are appointed by the federal government. |
| **Alberta Court of Justice** | Handles most criminal cases, plus family, youth, traffic and civil claims up to $100,000. Called the Provincial Court of Alberta until 2022. Its judges are appointed by the province. |

Appeals from the Court of Appeal go to the [Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court).
See [Courts](/learn/law/courts) for how the levels fit together.

## Local government

Municipalities in Alberta, such as cities, towns, villages, municipal districts
(counties) and specialized municipalities, are created under the **Municipal
Government Act**. Like all municipalities in Canada, they only have the powers the
province gives them. See [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities).

Since 2018, **Calgary and Edmonton** each have a **city charter**, a set of rules made
under the Municipal Government Act that gives the two big cities some extra powers and
flexibility.
