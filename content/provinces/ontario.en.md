## How it's governed

Ontario's legislature is the **Legislative Assembly of Ontario**, which meets at
**Queen's Park** in Toronto. People often use "Queen's Park" as shorthand for the
provincial government, the way "Ottawa" means the federal one. Ontario's elected
members are called **MPPs** (Members of Provincial Parliament) instead of MLAs.

Unlike most of the original provinces, Ontario never had an appointed upper house after
Confederation. Its legislature has had only one chamber since 1867.

The party that can keep the **confidence** (majority support) of the Assembly forms the
government, and its leader becomes **premier**. See
[Confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence).

The **Lieutenant Governor** represents the King. They give **royal assent** to bills
and carry out other formal duties on the advice of the elected government. See
[The Crown](/learn/crown).

## What makes it different

### The biggest province

Ontario has more people than any other province, nearly 4 in 10 Canadians. <!-- check: share of population against latest Statistics Canada estimate -->
That gives it the most seats in the federal House of Commons, because seats are
divided among the provinces mainly by population. It's also home to **Ottawa**, the
national capital, as well as Toronto, Canada's largest city.

### From Upper Canada to Ontario

Before Confederation, the area was the British colony of **Upper Canada** (created in
1791). In 1841 it was joined with Lower Canada (today's Quebec) to form the **Province
of Canada**, where it was known as **Canada West**. Confederation in 1867 split the
Province of Canada into two new provinces, Ontario and Quebec. See
[What's in the 1867 Act](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867).

### French-language services

Ontario has the largest French-speaking population outside Quebec, but it isn't
officially bilingual. Instead, the **French Language Services Act** (1986) gives
people the right to receive provincial government services in French at head offices
and in **designated areas**, which are places with significant francophone populations.
Complaints about French-language services are handled by Ontario's Ombudsman.
<!-- check: French Language Services Commissioner functions still within the Ombudsman's office -->

Compare this with [New Brunswick](/provinces/new-brunswick), which is officially
bilingual under the Constitution.

### Cities as "creatures of the province"

Ontario shows clearly that municipalities have no constitutional protection of their
own. In 2018, in the middle of a municipal election campaign, the province passed a
law cutting Toronto's city council from 47 seats to 25. In 2021, the Supreme Court of
Canada ruled in *Toronto (City) v. Ontario* that the province was allowed to do so. See
[Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities).

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Court of Appeal for Ontario** | The province's highest court. Hears appeals. |
| **Superior Court of Justice** | The **superior court**: serious criminal trials and larger civil and family cases. It includes the **Divisional Court**, which hears some appeals and reviews of government decisions, and the **Small Claims Court**. Its judges are appointed by the federal government. |
| **Ontario Court of Justice** | Handles most criminal cases, including youth cases, and many family matters. Its judges are appointed by the province. |

Above them is the [Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court). See
[Courts](/learn/law/courts).

## Local government

Most Ontario municipalities are governed by the **Municipal Act, 2001**. Toronto has its
own law, the **City of Toronto Act, 2006**, which gives it some broader powers.

Ontario municipalities come in three types:

- **Upper-tier** municipalities (counties and regions) handle services that cover a
  wide area.
- **Lower-tier** municipalities (cities, towns and townships inside a county or region)
  handle more local services.
- **Single-tier** municipalities, such as Toronto and Ottawa, do both.

> **As of 2026:** Since 2022, Ontario has given **"strong mayor" powers** to the mayors
> of a growing number of municipalities. These let a mayor, for example, propose the
> budget and hire senior staff. <!-- check: current list of municipalities and scope of powers -->
