## How it's governed

Yukon is a **[territory](term:territory)**, so its powers come from a federal law, the **Yukon Act**,
not from the Constitution. In principle Parliament could change them. In practice,
Yukon now runs most of the same things a province does. See
[Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories).

- **The Commissioner** is appointed by the federal cabinet and performs the formal
  duties a [Lieutenant Governor](org:lieutenant-governor) does in a province: giving assent to laws, opening the
  Legislature, appointing the cabinet. The role used to carry real power. Today the
  Commissioner acts on the advice of Yukon's elected government, and the Yukon Act
  describes Yukon as having **[responsible government](term:responsible-government)** "similar in principle to that
  of Canada."
- **The Legislature** is made up of the Commissioner and the Legislative Assembly,
  whose {{legislature.seats.yukon}} **[MLAs](term:mla)** pass Yukon's laws.
- **The Premier** is the leader who can keep the **[confidence](term:confidence)** of the Assembly,
  normally the leader of the party with the most seats, and chooses cabinet from among
  the MLAs.

## What makes it different

### Party politics, unlike the other two territories

Yukon works much like a small province. Candidates run for **political parties**,
and the party that can hold the Assembly's confidence forms the government. Party
politics began with the 1978 territorial election. This is the opposite of the
[Northwest Territories](/provinces/northwest-territories) and
[Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), which use **[consensus government](term:consensus-government)** with no parties.

### Born in a gold rush

Yukon was part of the vast Northwest Territories until the **Klondike Gold Rush**
brought tens of thousands of people north. In **1898** Parliament made it a separate
territory so it could be governed directly. Dawson City was the first capital;
the capital moved to Whitehorse in 1953.

### From appointed officials to self-government

For decades, the federally appointed Commissioner ran Yukon's government, with an
elected council in an advisory role. Over the 1970s that changed. In 1979, according to
Wikipedia's article on Yukon, a letter from the federal minister, Jake Epp, rather than
a new law, moved power from the Commissioner to the elected legislature. That set up
**responsible government** in Yukon. The current Yukon Act, which came into force in 2003, wrote
the arrangement into law.

### Devolution: the first territory to manage its own land

On **April 1, 2003**, Yukon became the first territory to take over the management
of its public lands, water, forests and minerals from the federal government. This
transfer, called **[devolution](term:devolution)**, came with the new Yukon Act. The Northwest
Territories [followed in 2014](event:northwest-territories-devolution-2014), and Nunavut's transfer is scheduled for 2027.

### Self-governing First Nations

Yukon is a national leader in [modern treaties](term:modern-treaty). In 1993, Canada, Yukon and the Council
for Yukon Indian People (now the Council of Yukon First Nations) signed the
**Umbrella Final Agreement**, a framework for individual land claim agreements.
Since then, **most of Yukon's First Nations** have signed final and
self-government agreements, according to Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern
Affairs Canada. These First Nations make their own laws on many matters
for their citizens and lands, alongside the territorial and federal governments. See
[Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government).

### Languages

Under Yukon's **Languages Act**, English and French can be used in the Legislature
and the courts, and laws are published in both. The Act also recognizes the importance
of Yukon's Indigenous languages, which can be used in Legislative Assembly debates.

## Courts

| Court | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Court of Appeal of Yukon** | Hears appeals from the Supreme Court and the Territorial Court. Its judges are drawn from the British Columbia Court of Appeal and from the courts of Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. |
| **Supreme Court of Yukon** | The **superior court**: serious criminal trials, larger civil cases, divorce. Despite the name, it is not the highest court. |
| **Territorial Court of Yukon** | Hears most criminal cases, youth cases and family matters. |
| **Small Claims Court** | Smaller civil disputes. |
| **Justices of the peace** | Minor offences and other limited matters. |

See [the court structure](/learn/law/courts).

## Local government

Yukon has eight incorporated municipalities, including the City of Whitehorse
and the City of Dawson. Most other communities are unincorporated, with local
services provided by the territorial government or by First Nations governments.

See [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities).
