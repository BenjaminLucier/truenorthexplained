## A

### Aboriginal {#aboriginal}
The term the Constitution Act, 1982 uses for Indigenous peoples, because it was the usual word in 1982. Section 35(2) says the "aboriginal peoples of Canada" include the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous), [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Aboriginal rights {#aboriginal-rights}
Rights that come from an Indigenous people's own practices and relationship to the land, such as a right to fish for food. Section 35 recognizes and affirms them; it didn't create them. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Aboriginal title {#aboriginal-title}
A right to the land itself, not just to particular activities on it. Courts accepted that it exists in *Calder* (1973) and first declared it over a specific area in *Tsilhqot'in* (2014); once title is established, the Crown needs the nation's consent to use the land or must meet a strict justification test. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Acadians {#acadians}
Descendants of the French settlers of the Maritime region. Most of New Brunswick's French speakers are Acadians, and Nova Scotia has electoral districts drawn to help Acadian communities elect a representative. See: [New Brunswick](/provinces/new-brunswick), [Nova Scotia](/provinces/nova-scotia)

### Accounting officer {#accounting-officer}
A role held by deputy ministers: they can be called before parliamentary committees to answer for how their department manages its money. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Act of Parliament {#act-of-parliament}
A bill that has passed the House of Commons and the Senate in the same words and received royal assent. Also called a statute. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Adjournment proceedings {#adjournment-proceedings}
Short evening exchanges in the House, Monday to Thursday, where an MP unhappy with an answer in Question Period can raise the issue again. Nicknamed "the late show". See: [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Administration of justice {#administration-of-justice}
A provincial power (s. 92(14) of the 1867 Act) to set up and run courts and handle civil procedure. It's why provinces run most courts, police forces and criminal prosecutions, even though criminal law itself is federal. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Administrative independence {#administrative-independence}
One of the three supports of judicial independence: courts, not governments, control matters that affect how they decide cases, such as which judge hears which case. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Administrative law {#administrative-law}
The law on how courts supervise government decisions. It's public law, so it follows common-law principles everywhere, including in Quebec. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Administrative tribunal {#administrative-tribunal}
A specialized body set up by law to decide particular kinds of disputes, such as refugee claims, landlord–tenant disputes or human rights complaints. Tribunals are less formal than courts, and a court can usually review their decisions. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Administrator of the Government of Canada {#administrator-of-the-government-of-canada}
The role the Chief Justice of Canada takes on when the Governor General is absent or the office is vacant, with the power to exercise the Governor General's powers. See: [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### Advance polls {#advance-polls}
Voting days held on the 10th, 9th, 8th and 7th days before a federal election day. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day)

### Advocate {#advocate}
In Quebec, a lawyer (*avocat*). Quebec's legal professionals are advocates and notaries rather than barristers and solicitors. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Agencies {#agencies}
Federal bodies such as the Canada Revenue Agency and Statistics Canada that are part of the government but do specialized work under their own legislation and structure. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Agent of the Crown {#agent-of-the-crown}
A body, such as most Crown corporations, that legally acts on the government's behalf and generally shares its privileges, such as not paying provincial or municipal taxes. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### Agents of Parliament {#agents-of-parliament}
See [Officers of Parliament](/glossary#officers-of-parliament).

### Alberta Act {#alberta-act}
The 1905 federal Act that created Alberta out of part of the North-West Territories, at the same time as Saskatchewan. See: [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan)

### Alternative vote {#alternative-vote}
See [Ranked ballot](/glossary#ranked-ballot).

### Amending formula {#amending-formula}
The rules for changing the written Constitution, in Part V of the Constitution Act, 1982 (ss. 38–49). It sets five procedures, from one legislature acting alone to Parliament plus all ten provinces, depending on what's being changed. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Appeal as of right {#appeal-as-of-right}
An appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada that doesn't need the Court's permission, available only in a few situations, such as certain criminal cases where a court of appeal judge dissented on a point of law. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court)

### Appropriation bill {#appropriation-bill}
See [Supply bill](/glossary#supply-bill).

### Assimilation {#assimilation}
The goal of much federal policy in the 19th and 20th centuries: absorbing Indigenous peoples into settler society. The Indian Act and residential schools were its main tools. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous), [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Asymmetry {#asymmetry}
The way federalism lets provinces use the same powers differently. Quebec, for example, uses civil law for private matters, runs its own pension plan and collects its own personal income tax. See: [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Auditor General {#auditor-general}
The officer of Parliament who audits the federal government's finances and programs and reports to Parliament, not to the government. Appointed for a single ten-year term after consulting every recognized party, with approval by both the House and the Senate. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs), [Government in action](/learn/government)

## B

### Band {#band}
A First Nations community recognized under the Indian Act. Reserves are set apart for the use and benefit of a band, and band membership (belonging to a particular First Nation) is separate from status. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Band council {#band-council}
An elected chief and councillors who run a reserve community, a model the Indian Act imposed. Today First Nations can hold council elections under the Indian Act, a custom code, or the *First Nations Elections Act*. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act), [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities)

### Bank of Canada {#bank-of-canada}
Canada's central bank, a special federal Crown corporation. It sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable, issues all banknotes and acts as the federal government's banker. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### BDC {#bdc}
See [Business Development Bank of Canada](/glossary#business-development-bank-of-canada).

### Big M Drug Mart {#big-m-drug-mart}
*R. v. Big M Drug Mart* (1985), where the Supreme Court struck down the federal *Lord's Day Act* because its purpose, forcing Sunday observance, violated freedom of religion. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Bijuralism {#bijuralism}
Having two legal traditions side by side. Canada uses the common law in nine provinces and the territories and the civil law for private matters in Quebec, so it is **bijural**. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Bill {#bill}
A proposed law. To become an Act of Parliament it must pass the House of Commons and the Senate in exactly the same words and then receive royal assent. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Bill 21 {#bill-21}
The common name for Quebec's 2019 law on state secularism, which bans some public employees from wearing religious symbols at work. It uses the notwithstanding clause, and that override has been renewed. See: [The notwithstanding clause: section 33](/learn/charter/notwithstanding-clause), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Bill 96 {#bill-96}
The common name for Quebec's 2022 law that significantly expanded the Charter of the French Language. It uses the notwithstanding clause. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec), [The notwithstanding clause: section 33](/learn/charter/notwithstanding-clause)

### Bill 101 {#bill-101}
See [Charter of the French Language](/glossary#charter-of-the-french-language).

### Bill C-31 {#bill-c-31}
The 1985 change to the Indian Act that removed the rule stripping status from First Nations women who married men without status, and restored status to many people. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### BNA Act {#bna-act}
See [British North America Act](/glossary#british-north-america-act).

### Branches of government {#branches-of-government}
The **legislative** branch (Parliament) makes laws, the **executive** (the government) carries them out, and the **judicial** branch (the courts) interprets them. In Canada the first two are fused, while the courts are independent of both. See: [The big picture](/learn/big-picture), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### British North America Act {#british-north-america-act}
The original name of the Constitution Act, 1867, used until 1982. See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Budget {#budget}
The government's economic and fiscal plan, presented by the Minister of Finance. It announces taxes and programs but doesn't by itself make spending legal; the vote on the budget motion is a confidence vote. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Budget implementation act {#budget-implementation-act}
A law that carries out the tax and other legal changes announced in a budget. Tax changes start with a **ways and means motion** in the House. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Business Development Bank of Canada {#business-development-bank-of-canada}
A federal Crown corporation (BDC) that provides financing and advice for small and medium businesses. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### By-election {#by-election}
An election to fill a single seat that becomes vacant between general elections, for example when an MP resigns or dies. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day)

## C

### Cabinet {#cabinet}
The Prime Minister and the ministers they choose, almost always MPs from the governing party. It is the government's main decision-making body and, in practice, the active part of the Privy Council. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections)

### Cabinet committees {#cabinet-committees}
Smaller groups of ministers that do much of cabinet's work in particular areas. The Treasury Board is one. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Cabinet solidarity {#cabinet-solidarity}
The convention, also called **collective responsibility**, that cabinet discussions are confidential and every minister must publicly support cabinet's decisions or resign. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)

### Calder {#calder}
*Calder* (1973), the Nisga'a case in which most of the Supreme Court accepted that Aboriginal title exists in Canadian law. It pushed Ottawa to start negotiating modern treaties. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties)

### Canada Act 1982 {#canada-act-1982}
The British law, passed at Canada's request, that enacted the Constitution Act, 1982 as a schedule and ended any further British power to legislate for Canada. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Canada Health Act {#canada-health-act}
The federal law setting five criteria provincial health insurance must meet to get the full Canada Health Transfer: public administration, comprehensiveness, universality, portability and accessibility. It also bans extra-billing and user charges. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Canada Health Transfer {#canada-health-transfer}
The largest federal transfer (CHT), paid to every province and territory on an equal per-person basis for health care, on condition that they follow the Canada Health Act. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation {#canada-mortgage-and-housing-corporation}
A federal Crown corporation (CMHC) that provides mortgage insurance and runs housing programs. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### Canada Post {#canada-post}
The federal Crown corporation that delivers mail and parcels. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### Canada Social Transfer {#canada-social-transfer}
A federal transfer (CST) paid to every province and territory on an equal per-person basis for post-secondary education, social assistance and social services, and early childhood development and child care. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization)

### Canadian Bill of Rights {#canadian-bill-of-rights}
A 1960 federal law protecting rights. Because it was an ordinary statute that applied only to federal laws, courts rarely used it to strike anything down; the Charter replaced it as the main rights guarantee. See: [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter), [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867)

### Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms {#canadian-charter-of-rights-and-freedoms}
See [Charter of Rights and Freedoms](/glossary#charter-of-rights-and-freedoms).

### Canadian Judicial Council {#canadian-judicial-council}
A body created by Parliament in 1971, made up of the chief justices and associate chief justices of the federally appointed courts. It investigates complaints about federally appointed judges and can recommend removal. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges)

### Caretaker convention {#caretaker-convention}
The convention that during an election campaign the government avoids major new decisions except where truly necessary. See: [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)

### Carter v. Canada {#carter-v-canada}
*Carter v. Canada (Attorney General)* (2015), where the Supreme Court unanimously struck down the total ban on assisted dying as a violation of s. 7. Parliament then passed a law on medical assistance in dying in 2016. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases), [Reasonable limits: section 1](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits)

### Case law {#case-law}
The rules that come out of court decisions. Judges interpret constitutions, statutes and regulations, and in common-law areas they built up whole fields of law case by case. See: [The legal system](/learn/law), [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Caucus {#caucus}
A party's group of MPs (or members of a legislature). Under the Reform Act, a caucus with 12 or more MPs votes after each election on whether to adopt rules letting it expel members or review the leader. See: [How parties choose leaders](/learn/elections/party-leaders), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### CBC/Radio-Canada {#cbc-radio-canada}
The federal Crown corporation that is Canada's public broadcaster in English and French. The *Broadcasting Act* protects its journalistic and programming independence. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### Central agencies {#central-agencies}
The organizations that coordinate the rest of the federal government: the Privy Council Office, the Treasury Board and its Secretariat, and the Department of Finance. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Central bank {#central-bank}
The institution that runs a country's monetary policy and issues its banknotes. In Canada that's the Bank of Canada, not the Royal Canadian Mint. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Charlottetown Accord {#charlottetown-accord}
A broad 1992 package of constitutional changes, including distinct-society recognition for Quebec, an elected Senate and an Indigenous right to self-government. Voters rejected it in a national referendum on October 26, 1992. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### Charlottetown Conference {#charlottetown-conference}
The September 1864 meeting in Charlottetown, the first of the meetings that led to Confederation. See: [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island)

### Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms (Quebec) {#quebec-charter-of-human-rights-and-freedoms}
Quebec's own rights charter, adopted in 1975. Unlike the Canadian Charter, it also applies between private parties, such as an employer and an employee. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Charter of Rights and Freedoms {#charter-of-rights-and-freedoms}
Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982 (ss. 1–34), which protects rights such as freedom of expression, the right to vote, legal rights and equality against **governments**. Rights can be limited under s. 1 and some can be overridden under s. 33. See: [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter), [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights)

### Charter of the French Language {#charter-of-the-french-language}
Quebec's 1977 language law, often called **Bill 101**. It makes French the normal language of government, work, business and public signs, and requires most children to attend French-language schools. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec), [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Chief Electoral Officer {#chief-electoral-officer}
The officer of Parliament who heads Elections Canada and reports to Parliament, not to the government. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day), [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Chief Justice of Canada {#chief-justice-of-canada}
The head of the Supreme Court of Canada, one of its nine judges. The Chief Justice also acts as Administrator of the Government when the Governor General is absent. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### CHT {#cht}
See [Canada Health Transfer](/glossary#canada-health-transfer).

### Circuit {#circuit}
Judges travelling to small communities to hold court where people live, common in the territories. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), [Northwest Territories](/provinces/northwest-territories)

### Citizen initiative {#citizen-initiative}
A petition process letting voters propose a law or policy question, which can go to the legislature or to a referendum if enough people sign. Alberta and British Columbia have full initiative laws. See: [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### City charter {#city-charter}
A province's special law or set of rules for one large city, giving it different or broader powers than other municipalities. Examples include the Vancouver Charter, The City of Winnipeg Charter, the Halifax Regional Municipality Charter, the City of Toronto Act, 2006 and the charters for Calgary and Edmonton. See: [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia), [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities)

### Civil Code of Québec {#civil-code-of-quebec}
The code that is the starting point for private law in Quebec. It came into force on January 1, 1994, replacing the Civil Code of Lower Canada of 1866. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Civil law {#civil-law}
The legal tradition from France and continental Europe, built around a written code of general principles. Quebec uses it for private law such as contracts, property and family. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Civil liability {#civil-liability}
Quebec's civil-law rules on harm caused by carelessness, set out in the Civil Code. The common-law equivalent is the law of torts. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Clarity Act {#clarity-act}
The federal law Parliament passed in 2000 following the Secession Reference. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Clerk of the Privy Council {#clerk-of-the-privy-council}
The country's top public servant: head of the Privy Council Office, head of the public service, the Prime Minister's deputy minister and secretary to the cabinet. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Closure {#closure}
See [Time allocation and closure](/glossary#time-allocation-and-closure).

### CMHC {#cmhc}
See [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](/glossary#canada-mortgage-and-housing-corporation).

### Coalition {#coalition}
An arrangement where two or more parties share cabinet seats. Coalitions have been very rare at the federal level in Canada. See: [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence), [Reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers)

### Collective responsibility {#collective-responsibility}
See [Cabinet solidarity](/glossary#cabinet-solidarity).

### Commander-in-chief {#commander-in-chief}
The formal head of the armed forces. The Constitution gives this role to the monarch (s. 15), and the Letters Patent give it to the Governor General. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### Commission of Government {#commission-of-government}
The appointed body Britain put in place to run Newfoundland from 1934 to 1949, after its legislature was suspended during the Great Depression. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador)

### Commissioner {#commissioner}
The formal head of each territory, appointed by the federal government. A Commissioner gives assent to territorial laws and acts on the advice of the elected territorial government, much like a Lieutenant Governor, but is legally a federal official rather than the King's representative. See: [Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories), [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### Commissioner of Official Languages {#commissioner-of-official-languages}
The federal officer of Parliament who oversees compliance with the *Official Languages Act*. New Brunswick has its own Commissioner of Official Languages who handles complaints there. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs), [New Brunswick](/provinces/new-brunswick)

### Commissioner of the Environment and Sustainable Development {#commissioner-of-the-environment-and-sustainable-development}
The official inside the Auditor General's office who carries out environmental audits. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Committee of the Whole {#committee-of-the-whole}
The entire House sitting as a committee, with less formal rules, sometimes used for spending bills. See: [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Committees {#committees}
Small groups of MPs or senators who study bills clause by clause, hear witnesses, examine spending and investigate issues. Types include **standing**, **legislative**, **special** and **joint** committees. See: [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees), [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Common law {#common-law}
The legal tradition inherited from England, where judges' past decisions (precedent) are a main source of law. It applies in nine provinces and the three territories, and also means the body of judge-made law itself. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Compensation commission {#compensation-commission}
An independent body whose recommendations governments must consider before changing judges' pay, a part of judges' financial security. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges)

### Concurrent powers {#concurrent-powers}
Subjects on which both Parliament and the provinces can make laws, such as agriculture and immigration (s. 95) and pensions (s. 94A). See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Confederation {#confederation}
The 1867 union of Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick into Canada. Other provinces joined or were created later, the most recent being Newfoundland and Labrador in 1949. See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### Confederation Bridge {#confederation-bridge}
The bridge linking Prince Edward Island to the mainland, opened in 1997. Replacing the ferry the Constitution promised took a 1993 amendment agreed by Parliament and PEI alone. See: [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island)

### Confidence {#confidence}
The support of a majority of the House of Commons (or a provincial legislature) on key votes. A government can stay in office only while it has the House's confidence; this is a convention, not written law. See: [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence), [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Confidence-and-supply agreement {#confidence-and-supply-agreement}
A deal in a minority Parliament where another party promises to support the government on confidence votes in exchange for policy commitments, while staying outside cabinet. See: [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence)

### Confidence vote {#confidence-vote}
A vote that tests whether the government still has the House's support: an explicit confidence motion, votes on the budget and supply, the vote on the Speech from the Throne, or any vote the government declares a matter of confidence. Losing one means the Prime Minister resigns or asks for an election. See: [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Conflict of Interest and Ethics Commissioner {#conflict-of-interest-and-ethics-commissioner}
The officer of Parliament who administers the *Conflict of Interest Act* for ministers and senior appointees and the Conflict of Interest Code for MPs. Senators have their own Senate Ethics Officer. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Consensus government {#consensus-government}
The system in the Northwest Territories and Nunavut: candidates run as independents with no political parties, and after an election the members choose the premier and cabinet from among themselves. See: [Northwest Territories](/provinces/northwest-territories), [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), [Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories)

### Consolidated Revenue Fund {#consolidated-revenue-fund}
The single fund into which federal revenues are paid (s. 102 of the 1867 Act), and from which the government draws money once Parliament approves it. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Constituency {#constituency}
See [Riding](/glossary#riding).

### Constitution {#constitution}
The set of rules that creates a country's institutions, says what each can do and limits them. Canada's is the supreme law and isn't one document: it includes the Constitution Acts of 1867 and 1982, other written texts, and unwritten conventions. See: [The Constitution](/learn/constitution), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Constitution Act, 1867 {#constitution-act-1867}
The British law that created Canada on July 1, 1867, originally called the British North America Act. It sets up the Crown, Parliament, the provinces and the courts, and divides law-making powers (ss. 91 and 92). See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Constitution Act, 1982 {#constitution-act-1982}
The Act that patriated the Constitution. It added the Charter (Part I), recognized Aboriginal and treaty rights (s. 35), set out the amending formula (Part V) and made the Constitution the supreme law (s. 52). See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Constitutional convention {#constitutional-convention}
See [Convention](/glossary#convention).

### Constitutional monarchy {#constitutional-monarchy}
A system where a monarch is head of state but, by convention, acts only on the advice of elected ministers. Canada's King "reigns but does not rule". See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Convention {#convention}
An unwritten rule of political behaviour that the people involved treat as binding, such as the rule that the government needs the House's confidence. Courts can recognize a convention but won't enforce it; breaking one has political, not legal, consequences. See: [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Cooperative federalism {#cooperative-federalism}
The modern courts' preference for letting federal and provincial laws operate side by side where they can, rather than looking for conflicts. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Council of the Federation {#council-of-the-federation}
The body through which the premiers meet on their own, created in 2003. See: [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Court martial {#court-martial}
A trial in the separate military justice system for members of the Canadian Armed Forces. Appeals go to the Court Martial Appeal Court of Canada and then to the Supreme Court. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Court of appeal {#court-of-appeal}
The highest court in each province and territory. It doesn't hold trials; it reviews lower-court decisions for legal errors, usually with three judges. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Court of King's Bench {#court-of-kings-bench}
The name several provinces, including Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and New Brunswick, use for their superior court. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts), [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan)

### Creature of the province {#creature-of-the-province}
Lawyers' description of municipalities: they exist only because a provincial law created them, and the province can change their powers, merge them or redraw their councils. See: [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities), [Ontario](/provinces/ontario)

### Criminal Code {#criminal-code}
The central federal criminal law, first passed in 1892. It defines most offences and sets out criminal procedure, and is the same in every province. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Criminal law {#criminal-law}
Law defining crimes and their procedure. Only Parliament can make it (s. 91(27)), but provinces run most of the policing, prosecuting and courts. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Crown {#crown}
The legal term for the state's executive authority, personified in the monarch. In Canadian law "the Crown" usually means the government in its legal capacity. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Crown corporation {#crown-corporation}
A company wholly owned by the government, with its own board and a mandate set by law, run at arm's length from day-to-day politics. Examples include Canada Post, CBC/Radio-Canada and, provincially, utilities such as Hydro-Québec. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations), [Government in action](/learn/government), [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan)

### Crown land {#crown-land}
Public land owned by the Crown. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan)

### Crown prosecutor {#crown-prosecutor}
A government lawyer who prosecutes crimes, also called **Crown counsel**. Cases are brought in the King's name. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### CST {#cst}
See [Canada Social Transfer](/glossary#canada-social-transfer).

### Cultural genocide {#cultural-genocide}
The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's description of the policy that the residential school system was part of. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Custom election code {#custom-election-code}
An election code designed by a First Nation community itself, one of the options for choosing a band council instead of Indian Act elections. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

## D

### Daniels {#daniels}
*Daniels* (2016), where the Supreme Court held that Métis and non-status Indians are "Indians" under s. 91(24), so they fall under federal jurisdiction. It decided which government is responsible, not any particular rights. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act {#declaration-on-the-rights-of-indigenous-peoples-act}
British Columbia's 2019 law (DRIPA), the first in Canada to commit a government to bring its laws into line with UNDRIP, with an action plan and yearly reports. See: [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### Delegate convention {#delegate-convention}
The older way of choosing party leaders, where local party associations sent delegates to vote in person. Most parties now let all members vote directly. See: [How parties choose leaders](/learn/elections/party-leaders)

### Delgamuukw {#delgamuukw}
*Delgamuukw* (1997), brought by Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en hereditary chiefs, where the Supreme Court said Aboriginal title is a right to the land itself and that oral histories must be given real weight as evidence. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Department {#department}
A part of the federal government responsible for a broad area of policy, such as health or justice, led politically by a minister and administratively by a deputy minister. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Department of Finance {#department-of-finance}
The central agency responsible for tax and economic policy, and for preparing the budget. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Deputy minister {#deputy-minister}
The most senior public servant in a department. Appointed by the Governor in Council on the Prime Minister's recommendation, they run the department and give the minister non-partisan advice. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Devolution {#devolution}
The federal government transferring province-like powers to a territorial government, especially control over public lands and natural resources. Yukon took them over in 2003, the Northwest Territories in 2014, and Nunavut's transfer is scheduled for 2027. See: [Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories), [Yukon](/provinces/yukon), [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

### Dialogue {#dialogue}
A name some people use for the back-and-forth between courts and legislatures, where a legislature responds to a Charter ruling by passing a revised law. See: [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter)

### Disallowance {#disallowance}
A power in the 1867 Act letting the federal government cancel a provincial law within a year. Last used in the 1940s and considered obsolete by most experts, though never formally repealed. See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867)

### Dissolution {#dissolution}
Ending a whole Parliament, which triggers an election. The Governor General dissolves Parliament, normally on the Prime Minister's advice. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day), [Reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers)

### Distinct society {#distinct-society}
Recognition of Quebec's distinctiveness that was central to both the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords, both of which failed. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Divisible Crown {#divisible-crown}
The idea that the same monarch is the Crown "in right of Canada" for federal matters and the Crown "in right of" each province for provincial matters, so each government acts in the King's name. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### Division of powers {#division-of-powers}
How the Constitution splits law-making between Parliament (mainly s. 91) and the provinces (mainly s. 92), with a few other sections adding shared or specific powers. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Dominion {#dominion}
A self-governing part of the British Empire. Newfoundland was a dominion in the early 20th century, with a status similar to Canada's at the time. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador)

### Double aspect {#double-aspect}
The court-made rule that some subjects have both a federal and a provincial side, so both orders can legislate. Impaired driving is both a crime and a reason to suspend a licence. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Douglas Treaties {#douglas-treaties}
Fourteen historic treaties signed on Vancouver Island in the 1850s, among the few historic treaties covering parts of British Columbia. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### DRIPA {#dripa}
See [Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act](/glossary#declaration-on-the-rights-of-indigenous-peoples-act).

### Duty to consult {#duty-to-consult}
The Crown's duty, set out in *Haida Nation* (2004), to consult Indigenous peoples, and where appropriate accommodate them, before acting in ways that could harm Aboriginal or treaty rights, even unproven ones. It comes from the honour of the Crown and isn't a veto. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

## E

### EDC {#edc}
See [Export Development Canada](/glossary#export-development-canada).

### Elections Canada {#elections-canada}
The independent, non-partisan agency that runs federal elections, by-elections and referendums, registers parties and enforces political financing rules. It's headed by the Chief Electoral Officer. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day), [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections)

### Electoral district {#electoral-district}
See [Riding](/glossary#riding).

### Enfranchisement {#enfranchisement}
An Indian Act policy under which a status person who, for example, became a lawyer or university graduate could lose status. It was presented as a path to citizenship and the vote. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Equality rights {#equality-rights}
Section 15 of the Charter: every individual is equal before and under the law, without discrimination on listed grounds or similar ones courts have added, such as sexual orientation. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Equalization {#equalization}
An unconditional federal transfer paid only to provinces whose fiscal capacity is below the national standard, out of federal general revenues. Section 36(2) of the 1982 Act commits Ottawa to the principle; provinces don't pay into it. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Estimates {#estimates}
The government's detailed requests to Parliament for the money each department needs, prepared by the Treasury Board. The **Main Estimates** cover the coming fiscal year; **Supplementary Estimates** add requests during the year. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Executive {#executive}
The branch of government that carries out the laws: in practice the Prime Minister and cabinet, acting formally in the King's name. See: [Government in action](/learn/government), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Exempt staff {#exempt-staff}
Ministers' political advisers and communications staff, hired outside the merit system, who leave with their minister. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Existing (section 35) {#existing-section-35}
In s. 35, rights that hadn't been lawfully ended (extinguished) before 1982. The word doesn't freeze rights in their 1982 form. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Export Development Canada {#export-development-canada}
A federal Crown corporation (EDC) that provides financing and insurance for exporters. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

## F

### Federal Court {#federal-court}
A court created by Parliament to handle areas it assigns, such as immigration and refugee decisions, intellectual property, maritime law and reviews of federal tribunals. Appeals go to the **Federal Court of Appeal**. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Federal parliamentary democracy {#federal-parliamentary-democracy}
The one-phrase description of Canada's system: power is divided between federal and provincial governments, and voters elect a Parliament from which the government is formed. See: [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Federation {#federation}
A country where power is divided between a central government and regional governments, and the division is protected by the constitution. Canada's two **orders of government** are federal and provincial. See: [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Fiduciary duty {#fiduciary-duty}
A trust-like duty. The Supreme Court described the Crown's fiduciary duties toward First Nations in *Guerin* (1984). See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Financial security {#financial-security}
One of the three supports of judicial independence: judges' pay is set by law, and governments can't cut it to punish judges. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### First Nations {#first-nations}
One of the three Indigenous groups the Constitution recognizes: many distinct nations, such as the Cree, Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee, Mi'kmaq, Haida and Dene. The Indian Act applies to First Nations, not to Inuit or Métis. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous), [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### First-past-the-post {#first-past-the-post}
Canada's electoral system (FPTP), also called **single-member plurality**: each riding elects one MP, and the candidate with the most votes wins, even without a majority. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post), [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections)

### Fiscal capacity {#fiscal-capacity}
How much revenue a province could raise if it applied national average tax rates, the measure used to decide who gets equalization. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization)

### Fiscal imbalance {#fiscal-imbalance}
The "vertical" gap between the federal government, which raises more revenue than its own jobs need, and the provinces, which carry expensive jobs like health care and education. See: [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Fiscal policy {#fiscal-policy}
Decisions about taxes and government spending, made by the government and Parliament, not by the Bank of Canada. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)

### Fiscal year {#fiscal-year}
The federal government's financial year, which runs from April 1 to March 31. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Fixed election date {#fixed-election-date}
A 2007 change to the Canada Elections Act setting federal elections for the third Monday of October in the fourth calendar year after the last one. It keeps the Governor General's power to dissolve Parliament, so early elections can still happen. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day), [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections)

### Ford v. Quebec {#ford-v-quebec}
*Ford v. Quebec (Attorney General)* (1988), where the Supreme Court held that commercial signs are protected expression and set the rules for using the notwithstanding clause: form only, no justification needed, no retroactivity. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases), [The notwithstanding clause: section 33](/learn/charter/notwithstanding-clause)

### FPIC {#fpic}
See [Free, prior and informed consent](/glossary#free-prior-and-informed-consent).

### FPTP {#fptp}
See [First-past-the-post](/glossary#first-past-the-post).

### Free, prior and informed consent {#free-prior-and-informed-consent}
A principle in UNDRIP (FPIC). People disagree about whether it amounts to something close to a right to say no to projects or a strong duty to seek agreement. See: [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### Free vote {#free-vote}
A vote where a party lets its MPs vote as they personally choose, usually on moral questions. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Fundamental freedoms {#fundamental-freedoms}
Section 2 of the Charter: freedom of conscience and religion; of thought, belief, opinion and expression; of peaceful assembly; and of association. They belong to everyone. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter)

## G

### General amending procedure {#general-amending-procedure}
The "7/50" rule (s. 38): an amendment needs the Senate, the House of Commons and at least seven provinces with at least 50% of the population of all the provinces. It covers most significant changes. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Government {#government}
In the everyday Canadian sense, the executive: the Prime Minister and cabinet, who sit in Parliament but aren't the same thing as Parliament. See: [Government in action](/learn/government), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Government bill {#government-bill}
A bill introduced by a minister. Most bills that become law are government bills. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Governor General {#governor-general}
The King's representative at the federal level, who exercises almost all of his powers in Canada. Appointed on the Prime Minister's advice, usually for about five years, and acts on ministers' advice except in rare reserve-power situations. See: [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general), [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### Governor in Council {#governor-in-council}
The Governor General acting on the advice of the federal cabinet. Many regulations and appointments are formally made this way. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Government in action](/learn/government), [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges)

## H

### Habeas corpus {#habeas-corpus}
The right, under s. 10 of the Charter, to have a court check whether your detention is lawful. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights)

### Haida Nation {#haida-nation}
*Haida Nation* (2004), the case that set out the Crown's duty to consult, and where appropriate accommodate, before acting in ways that could harm Aboriginal rights or title, even before they're proven. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Head of government {#head-of-government}
The person who leads the government: the Prime Minister federally, and a premier in each province and territory. See: [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Head of state {#head-of-state}
The formal top of the state. In Canada it's the King, not the Governor General or the Prime Minister. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Heads of power {#heads-of-power}
The "classes of subjects" listed in ss. 91 and 92, each a short phrase like "Banking" or "Hospitals". See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Hereditary chiefs {#hereditary-chiefs}
Traditional leaders, chosen within house and clan systems in many West Coast nations, whose authority predates the Indian Act's band councils. See: [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government), [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Honour of the Crown {#honour-of-the-crown}
The duty, described by the courts, that governments must deal honourably with Indigenous peoples, including in making and carrying out treaties and in consultation. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### House of Assembly {#house-of-assembly}
The name of the legislature in Nova Scotia and in Newfoundland and Labrador. See: [Nova Scotia](/provinces/nova-scotia), [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador)

### House of Commons {#house-of-commons}
The elected chamber of Parliament, with one MP per riding. It's the more powerful chamber: money bills start there, and the government stays in office only while it has the House's confidence. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence)

### Human rights laws {#human-rights-laws}
Federal and provincial laws that protect people against discrimination by private parties, such as employers and landlords, in areas the Charter doesn't reach. See: [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter), [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Hybrid offence {#hybrid-offence}
A criminal offence where the prosecutor chooses whether to proceed summarily or by indictment, such as assault and impaired driving. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

## I

### Independent {#independent}
A candidate or member who belongs to no political party. In the Northwest Territories and Nunavut every candidate runs as an independent. See: [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections), [Consensus government](/glossary#consensus-government)

### Independent Advisory Board for Senate Appointments {#independent-advisory-board-for-senate-appointments}
The board, used since 2016, that reviews applications and gives the Prime Minister a non-binding shortlist for each Senate vacancy, based on merit-based criteria. It's set by government policy, not law. See: [The Senate](/learn/parliament/senate)

### Independent Advisory Board (Supreme Court) {#independent-advisory-board-supreme-court}
The board, used since 2016, that gives the Prime Minister a non-binding shortlist of three to five functionally bilingual candidates for each Supreme Court vacancy. It's a government policy, not a constitutional requirement. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges)

### Indian {#indian}
A legal term still used in the Indian Act and in s. 91(24) of the 1867 Act. Courts have held that s. 91(24) covers Inuit, Métis and non-status Indians; in everyday speech most people say "First Nations". See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous), [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Indian Act {#indian-act}
A federal law first passed in 1876 under s. 91(24). It defines status, governs reserves and set up band councils, was a tool of assimilation for much of its history, and still applies to First Nations today. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Indian Register {#indian-register}
The federal list of people with status under the Indian Act. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Indictable offence {#indictable-offence}
A more serious criminal offence, with fuller procedure and higher maximum penalties, such as murder or robbery. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### Indigenous peoples {#indigenous-peoples}
The collective name for the original peoples of North America and their descendants. The Constitution recognizes three groups in Canada: First Nations, Inuit and Métis. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Inflation target {#inflation-target}
The core of the monetary policy framework the Bank of Canada and the federal government agree on every five years: 2%, the midpoint of a 1% to 3% range. The Bank decides independently how to hit it. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)

### Information Commissioner {#information-commissioner}
The officer of Parliament who oversees access to government records, and who can order departments to release records. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Inherent jurisdiction {#inherent-jurisdiction}
The power of superior courts to hear any case unless a law specifically gives it to another court. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Inherent right of self-government {#inherent-right-of-self-government}
Indigenous nations' right to govern themselves, which they see as one they've always had, not one granted by Canada. Since 1995 the federal government has recognized it as an existing right under s. 35. See: [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Interim supply {#interim-supply}
An advance of money Parliament normally grants in March to cover the first months of the fiscal year, before the Main Estimates are fully approved. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Inuit {#inuit}
The Indigenous people of the Arctic (one person is an *Inuk*), one of the three groups the Constitution recognizes. Courts held in 1939 that they fall under federal jurisdiction; the Indian Act doesn't apply to them. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous), [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

### Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit {#inuit-qaujimajatuqangit}
A term usually translated as Inuit traditional knowledge and values, which the Government of Nunavut tries to reflect in how it works. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

### Inuktut {#inuktut}
The common name for the Inuit language, which covers Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun. It is an official language of Nunavut. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

## J

### Joint address {#joint-address}
A request by both the Senate and the House of Commons, needed before the Governor General can remove a superior court judge. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges), [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Jordan {#jordan}
*R. v. Jordan* (2016), where the Supreme Court set ceilings for criminal trial delay under s. 11(b): 18 months in provincial court and 30 months in superior court, beyond which delay is presumed unreasonable. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Judicial Advisory Committees {#judicial-advisory-committees}
Committees in each province and territory, made up of people from the legal community and the public, that assess applicants for federal judicial appointments. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges)

### Judicial Committee of the Privy Council {#judicial-committee-of-the-privy-council}
A court in London that was Canada's final court of appeal until 1949. Criminal appeals to it ended in 1933. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867)

### Judicial independence {#judicial-independence}
The principle that judges decide cases only on the facts and the law, free from pressure. It rests on security of tenure, financial security and administrative independence. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Judicial review {#judicial-review}
A court ruling that a law that conflicts with the Constitution is of no force or effect, either because it's outside that government's powers or because it violates a protected right. The term also means courts reviewing officials' and tribunals' decisions. See: [The legal system](/learn/law), [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court)

### Justice of the peace {#justice-of-the-peace}
A judicial officer who handles minor offences and matters such as bail hearings, especially in small communities. See: [Northwest Territories](/provinces/northwest-territories), [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), [Yukon](/provinces/yukon)

## K

### King of Canada {#king-of-canada}
Canada's head of state. The same person as the King of the United Kingdom, but the two roles are legally separate, and for Canadian matters the monarch acts on Canadian advice. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### King–Byng affair {#king-byng-affair}
The 1926 crisis when Governor General Lord Byng refused Prime Minister Mackenzie King's request to dissolve Parliament. It's the classic federal test of the reserve powers. See: [Reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers), [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)

## L

### Land acknowledgement {#land-acknowledgement}
A statement at the start of an event recognizing the Indigenous nations whose land it takes place on, common partly because much land is unceded. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Land claims agreement {#land-claims-agreement}
See [Modern treaty](/glossary#modern-treaty).

### Leader of the Opposition {#leader-of-the-opposition}
The leader of the Official Opposition, who asks the first questions in Question Period. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Leadership review {#leadership-review}
A vote by party members, often after an election loss, on whether to keep the leader. Under the Reform Act, a caucus can also adopt a rule letting MPs trigger a review. See: [How parties choose leaders](/learn/elections/party-leaders)

### Leave to appeal {#leave-to-appeal}
The Supreme Court's permission to hear an appeal, needed in most cases. It's granted for issues of public importance, and only a small share of applications succeed. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Legal rights {#legal-rights}
Sections 7 to 14 of the Charter, which matter most when dealing with police and the criminal justice system: life, liberty and security of the person, protection from unreasonable search, the right to a lawyer, the presumption of innocence and more. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### Legal tender {#legal-tender}
Money that must be accepted to pay a debt. Coins are legal tender under the federal *Currency Act*, and legal tender is a federal power (s. 91(20)). See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)

### LEGISinfo {#legisinfo}
Parliament's website showing every bill's text and current stage. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Legislative Assembly {#legislative-assembly}
The usual name for a provincial or territorial legislature. Every provincial and territorial legislature has a single chamber. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### Legislative Council {#legislative-council}
An appointed upper house that several provinces once had and later abolished, from Manitoba in 1876 to Quebec, the last, in 1968. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec), [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba), [Nova Scotia](/provinces/nova-scotia)

### Letters Patent, 1947 {#letters-patent-1947}
The document, issued by King George VI, that authorizes the Governor General to exercise the monarch's powers in respect of Canada and makes the Governor General commander-in-chief. See: [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### Lieutenant Governor {#lieutenant-governor}
The King's representative in a province. Appointed by the Governor General on the federal government's advice, usually for about five years, but acts on the advice of the provincial government and doesn't report to the Governor General. See: [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general), [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### List proportional representation {#list-proportional-representation}
An electoral system where voters choose parties and seats are shared by vote share. It's closely proportional but has no local MPs. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### Living tree {#living-tree}
The standard approach to reading the Constitution, from the 1929 Persons Case: the Act is "a living tree capable of growth and expansion within its natural limits", applied to new things rather than frozen in 1867. See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867), [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Local service district {#local-service-district}
In some provinces, a small unincorporated area that gets basic services such as fire protection or water. In Newfoundland and Labrador they're run by elected committees; New Brunswick merged many of its into new local governments in 2023. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador), [New Brunswick](/provinces/new-brunswick)

### Loyalists {#loyalists}
Colonists who stayed loyal to Britain during the American Revolution. Many settled in what became New Brunswick. See: [New Brunswick](/provinces/new-brunswick)

## M

### Machinery of government {#machinery-of-government}
How cabinet and the departments are organized: creating portfolios, merging departments or moving responsibilities. The Prime Minister decides it. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Majority government {#majority-government}
A government whose party holds more than half the seats in the House, so it can pass its bills and survive confidence votes as long as its MPs vote together. See: [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Manitoba Act, 1870 {#manitoba-act-1870}
The law that made Manitoba a province, the result of the Red River Resistance. It's part of the Constitution; s. 23 requires laws in English and French and s. 31 set aside land for Métis children. See: [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba)

### Manufactured majority {#manufactured-majority}
A majority of seats won with well under half the vote, a common result of first-past-the-post. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### Mayor {#mayor}
The leader of a municipal council, usually elected directly by voters; sometimes called a reeve or warden. See: [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities)

### Medical assistance in dying {#medical-assistance-in-dying}
The federal law Parliament passed in 2016 after *Carter v. Canada* struck down the total ban on assisted dying. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Medicare {#medicare}
Canada's public health insurance, which began in Saskatchewan with hospital insurance (1947) and medical care insurance (1962). See: [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan), [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization)

### Meech Lake Accord {#meech-lake-accord}
A 1987 agreement between Ottawa and all ten premiers, built around Quebec's five conditions, including recognition as a distinct society. It died when not every legislature approved it by June 23, 1990. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Member of Parliament {#member-of-parliament}
An MP: a member of the House of Commons, elected by one riding. You vote for your local MP, not for the Prime Minister. See: [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Merit {#merit}
The principle, under the *Public Service Employment Act*, that public service appointments are based on qualifications and free from political influence. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Métis {#metis}
A distinct Indigenous people who emerged from relationships between First Nations and European fur traders, with their own culture, language (Michif) and nationhood. Mixed ancestry alone doesn't make someone Métis; the Indian Act doesn't apply to them. See: [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous), [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba)

### Métis Settlements {#metis-settlements}
Eight Métis communities in Alberta that hold land collectively under the *Metis Settlements Act* (1990) and have their own elected councils. See: [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### MHA {#mha}
Member of the House of Assembly, the title of elected members in Newfoundland and Labrador. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### Minimal impairment {#minimal-impairment}
See [Oakes test](/glossary#oakes-test).

### Minister {#minister}
A member of cabinet, almost always an MP, who sets a department's direction and answers for it in Parliament. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Ministerial responsibility {#ministerial-responsibility}
The convention that each minister answers to Parliament for everything their department does, even decisions they didn't personally make. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)

### Minority government {#minority-government}
A government whose party has less than half the seats, so it needs votes from at least one other party to pass bills and survive confidence votes. It has the same legal powers as a majority. See: [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Minority language education rights {#minority-language-education-rights}
Section 23 of the Charter: citizens in the English-speaking minority in Quebec, or the French-speaking minority elsewhere, can have their children educated in that language with public funds, where numbers warrant. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter)

### Mixed-member proportional {#mixed-member-proportional}
An electoral system (MMP) where some MPs are elected in ridings and extra "list" MPs are added so each party's seats match its vote share. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post), [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island)

### MLA {#mla}
Member of the Legislative Assembly, the usual title of elected members in most provinces and the territories. See: [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### MMP {#mmp}
See [Mixed-member proportional](/glossary#mixed-member-proportional).

### MNA {#mna}
Member of the National Assembly (*député*), the title of elected members in Quebec. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### Mobility rights {#mobility-rights}
Section 6 of the Charter: citizens can enter, stay in and leave Canada, and citizens and permanent residents can move to and earn a living in any province. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights)

### Modern treaty {#modern-treaty}
A treaty, also called a **comprehensive land claims agreement**, negotiated since 1975 where Aboriginal title was never dealt with. Rights in them are constitutionally protected treaty rights under s. 35(3). See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### Monetary policy {#monetary-policy}
Keeping inflation low, stable and predictable, mainly by setting the policy interest rate. The Bank of Canada runs it independently. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)

### Money bill {#money-bill}
A bill to tax or to spend public money. It must start in the House of Commons (s. 53), and new spending needs a royal recommendation (s. 54). See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Morgentaler {#morgentaler}
*R. v. Morgentaler* (1988), where the Supreme Court struck down the Criminal Code's abortion provisions as a violation of security of the person (s. 7). See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### MP {#mp}
See [Member of Parliament](/glossary#member-of-parliament).

### MPP {#mpp}
Member of Provincial Parliament, the title of elected members in Ontario. See: [Ontario](/provinces/ontario), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### MRC {#mrc}
See [Regional county municipality](/glossary#regional-county-municipality).

### Multijural {#multijural}
A description some scholars and courts use for Canada, recognizing Indigenous legal orders alongside the common law and civil law. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Municipality {#municipality}
A local government, such as a city, town, village or county, that handles things like roads, water and zoning. Municipalities are created by provincial law and have only the powers their province gives them. See: [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

## N

### National Assembly {#national-assembly}
Quebec's legislature, the only one in Canada with that name. It was called the Legislative Assembly until 1968. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### National concern {#national-concern}
A branch of the federal peace, order and good government power covering a distinct new matter the provinces can't handle alone. The Supreme Court used it in 2021 to uphold federal minimum carbon pricing. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Natural Resources Transfer Agreements {#natural-resources-transfer-agreements}
The 1930 agreements that gave Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Alberta control of their Crown lands and natural resources, which Ottawa had kept until then. They were made part of the Constitution. See: [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan), [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba)

### Night of the Long Knives {#night-of-the-long-knives}
The name often used in Quebec for the November 1981 talks where Ottawa and nine provinces reached the patriation deal overnight without Quebec's delegation. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982)

### Nisga'a Final Agreement {#nisgaa-final-agreement}
The first modern treaty in British Columbia, in effect since 2000, a direct result of the Nisga'a's long effort that included *Calder*. It set up the Nisga'a Lisims Government. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Non-status {#non-status}
A First Nations person who isn't registered under the Indian Act, often because of past rules in the Act. *Daniels* held that non-status Indians fall under s. 91(24). See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act), [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Notary (Quebec) {#notary-quebec}
In Quebec, a trained legal professional (*notaire*) who handles real estate deals, wills and contracts, unlike a common-law notary public. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Notwithstanding clause {#notwithstanding-clause}
Section 33 of the Charter, which lets Parliament or a provincial legislature declare that a law operates despite the fundamental freedoms (s. 2), legal rights (ss. 7–14) or equality rights (s. 15). Each use expires after five years unless re-enacted. See: [The notwithstanding clause: section 33](/learn/charter/notwithstanding-clause), [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter)

### Numbered Treaties {#numbered-treaties}
Eleven historic treaties signed from 1871 to 1921, after Confederation, covering land from northern Ontario across the Prairies to parts of BC, the Northwest Territories and Yukon. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [Saskatchewan](/provinces/saskatchewan)

### Nunatsiavut Government {#nunatsiavut-government}
An Inuit regional government in Labrador, created under the Labrador Inuit Land Claims Agreement, which took effect in 2005. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### Nunavummiut {#nunavummiut}
The people of Nunavut. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

### Nunavut Agreement {#nunavut-agreement}
The 1993 modern treaty between the Inuit of the eastern Arctic and the Crown, formerly called the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement. The largest land claim settlement in Canada's history, it led to the creation of Nunavut in 1999. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties)

### Nunavut Court of Justice {#nunavut-court-of-justice}
Nunavut's single-level trial court, which is both the superior court and the territorial court, so one court hears every kind of case. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated {#nunavut-tunngavik-incorporated}
The organization (NTI) that represents Inuit in making sure the Nunavut Agreement is carried out. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

## O

### Oakes test {#oakes-test}
The test from *R. v. Oakes* (1986) for justifying a limit on a Charter right under s. 1: a pressing and substantial objective, a rational connection, minimal impairment of the right, and benefits that outweigh the harms. The government must pass every step. See: [Reasonable limits: section 1](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits), [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Of no force or effect {#of-no-force-or-effect}
What happens to a law that conflicts with the Constitution, to the extent of the conflict, under s. 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982. See: [The Constitution](/learn/constitution), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions {#office-of-the-superintendent-of-financial-institutions}
The federal body (OSFI) that supervises individual banks for safety, a job the Bank of Canada doesn't do. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint)

### Officers of Parliament {#officers-of-parliament}
Independent watchdogs, also called **agents of Parliament**, created by law to help Parliament oversee the government. They report directly to Parliament, and Parliament is involved in appointing and removing them. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Official languages {#official-languages}
English and French, the official languages of Canada and of New Brunswick, with rights in Charter ss. 16–22 to use them in Parliament, federal courts and federal services. Some provinces and territories have their own official languages. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [New Brunswick](/provinces/new-brunswick), [Northwest Territories](/provinces/northwest-territories)

### Official Opposition {#official-opposition}
By convention, the party with the second-most seats in the House. Its leader is the Leader of the Opposition. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Ombudsperson {#ombudsperson}
An official who handles complaints for a particular group or about government services. Federal ombuds usually report to a minister, not Parliament; most provinces have one who reports to the legislature. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Opposition days {#opposition-days}
Days, also called allotted days, when opposition parties choose the topic of debate in the House, as a reminder that the House can refuse supply. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Orders in council {#orders-in-council}
Formal decisions of the Governor in Council, used to make regulations and many appointments. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### Orders of government {#orders-of-government}
The usual Canadian term for the federal and provincial governments, preferred over "levels" because neither is above the other. See: [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### OSFI {#osfi}
See [Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions](/glossary#office-of-the-superintendent-of-financial-institutions).

## P

### Paramountcy {#paramountcy}
The rule that when a valid federal law and a valid provincial law truly conflict, the federal law prevails and the provincial law stops applying to the extent of the conflict. Pensions (s. 94A) are the exception. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Parliament {#parliament}
Canada's federal law-making body, made up of the King (represented by the Governor General), the Senate and the House of Commons (s. 17). "A Parliament" also means the period from one election to the next. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [The big picture](/learn/big-picture)

### Parliament of Canada Act {#parliament-of-canada-act}
The federal law that, after a 2015 amendment often called the **Reform Act**, makes each party caucus with 12 or more MPs vote on rules for expulsion, choosing a chair, leadership reviews and interim leaders. See: [How parties choose leaders](/learn/elections/party-leaders)

### Parliamentary Budget Officer {#parliamentary-budget-officer}
The officer of Parliament (PBO) who gives MPs and senators independent analysis of the economy, the government's finances and the estimates, and costs proposals and election promises. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs), [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Parliamentary democracy {#parliamentary-democracy}
A system where voters elect a legislature and the government is formed from it and must keep its confidence. Canada uses the British-style Westminster version. See: [The big picture](/learn/big-picture), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Parliamentary secretary {#parliamentary-secretary}
An MP who assists a minister, especially in the House, but isn't a minister. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Party discipline {#party-discipline}
The strong tendency of Canadian MPs to vote with their party, enforced by whips. It's especially tight on confidence votes. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Patriation {#patriation}
Bringing the Constitution "home" in 1982, so that it could be amended in Canada and the British Parliament no longer had any role. Nine provinces agreed; Quebec did not. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Patriation Reference {#patriation-reference}
The 1981 Supreme Court opinion that Ottawa could legally ask Britain to amend the Constitution without the provinces, but that a convention required a "substantial degree" of provincial consent. It also set the test for identifying conventions. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)

### PBO {#pbo}
See [Parliamentary Budget Officer](/glossary#parliamentary-budget-officer).

### PCO {#pco}
See [Privy Council Office](/glossary#privy-council-office).

### Peace and Friendship Treaties {#peace-and-friendship-treaties}
Historic treaties of the 1700s with the Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik and Peskotomuhkati in the Maritimes and Gaspé. They did not surrender land; *R. v. Marshall* (1999) held they protect a Mi'kmaq right to fish and trade for a moderate livelihood. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [Nova Scotia](/provinces/nova-scotia)

### Peace, order and good government {#peace-order-and-good-government}
Parliament's general power (POGG) at the start of s. 91 to make laws on all matters not given exclusively to the provinces. It's the federal **residual power**, applied to gaps, matters of national concern and emergencies. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Penitentiary {#penitentiary}
A federal prison, run by Correctional Service Canada, where sentences of two years or more are served (s. 91(28)). See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Persons Case {#persons-case}
*Edwards v. Canada (Attorney General)* (1929), where the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council held that women were "persons" who could be appointed to the Senate and described the Constitution as a living tree. See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867), [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court)

### Pith and substance {#pith-and-substance}
What a law is mainly about. Courts use it to decide which head of power a law falls under; a valid law can still affect other subjects along the way. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Plebiscite {#plebiscite}
A non-binding public vote. See: [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island)

### Plurality {#plurality}
The most votes, even if short of half. Under first-past-the-post a plurality wins the riding. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### PMO {#pmo}
See [Prime Minister's Office](/glossary#prime-ministers-office).

### POGG {#pogg}
See [Peace, order and good government](/glossary#peace-order-and-good-government).

### Policy interest rate {#policy-interest-rate}
The key interest rate the Bank of Canada sets to steer inflation. Commercial banks' rates tend to follow it. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Pre-study {#pre-study}
The Senate studying a bill's subject while the bill is still in the House, to speed things up later. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Precedent {#precedent}
The common-law principle (*stare decisis*, "stand by things decided") that courts follow earlier decisions on similar facts, and that higher courts' decisions bind lower ones. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Premier {#premier}
The head of government in a province or territory: whoever can keep the confidence of the legislature, normally the leader of the largest party. In NWT and Nunavut the members choose the premier. See: [Provinces and territories](/provinces), [How parties choose leaders](/learn/elections/party-leaders)

### Prescribed by law {#prescribed-by-law}
The s. 1 requirement that a limit on a Charter right have a legal basis, such as a statute, regulation or common-law rule, and not be too vague. See: [Reasonable limits: section 1](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits)

### Presumption of innocence {#presumption-of-innocence}
The right of anyone charged with an offence to be presumed innocent until proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt (s. 11(d) of the Charter). See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [Reasonable limits: section 1](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits)

### Prime Minister {#prime-minister}
Canada's head of government: whoever can win and keep the confidence of the House of Commons, normally the leader of the party with the most seats. The office is barely mentioned in the written Constitution and rests almost entirely on convention. See: [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections), [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Prime Minister's Office {#prime-ministers-office}
The Prime Minister's own partisan political staff (PMO), led by the chief of staff. They aren't public servants and generally leave when the Prime Minister does. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Principles of fundamental justice {#principles-of-fundamental-justice}
The basic fairness that s. 7 of the Charter requires before anyone can be deprived of life, liberty or security of the person. See: [Your Charter rights, in plain language](/learn/charter/your-rights), [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Private bill {#private-bill}
A bill that benefits a specific person or organization, such as special powers for one company. Rare today. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Private law {#private-law}
Law on relationships between people: contracts, property, family, inheritance and liability for harm. It's the only area where Quebec's civil law differs from the rest of Canada. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Private member's bill {#private-members-bill}
A bill introduced by an MP who isn't a minister. These get limited House time, with order set by a random draw, so most never reach a final vote. Senators can introduce **Senate public bills** in a similar way. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Privatization {#privatization}
Selling a Crown corporation to private investors, as happened with Air Canada, Petro-Canada and Canadian National Railway. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### Privy Council {#privy-council}
The King's Privy Council for Canada, the advisory body created by s. 11 of the 1867 Act. Ministers join it for life; cabinet is its active part. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions)

### Privy Council Office {#privy-council-office}
The Prime Minister's non-partisan public service department (PCO), led by the Clerk of the Privy Council. It supports cabinet, gives policy advice and coordinates departments. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo), [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Proclamation {#proclamation}
The formal act by which the Governor General brings a constitutional amendment into effect, once the required resolutions have passed. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula)

### Property and civil rights {#property-and-civil-rights}
The broad provincial power in s. 92(13). "Civil rights" here means private legal relationships, such as contracts, property and most employment and business regulation, not civil liberties. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Prorogation {#prorogation}
Ending a session of Parliament without an election. Most bills that haven't passed die. The 2008 prorogation, granted to avoid an imminent confidence vote, is the best-known disputed case. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers)

### Provincial court {#provincial-court}
The trial court created and staffed by each province or territory (a **territorial court** in the territories). It hears most criminal cases, youth cases, traffic offences, many family matters and often small claims, with no juries. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Provincial offence {#provincial-offence}
A penalty attached to a provincial law, such as a speeding ticket, sometimes called a regulatory or quasi-criminal offence. It isn't a crime and doesn't give you a criminal record. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Provisional government {#provisional-government}
A temporary government set up until a permanent one exists, such as the one the Métis formed at Red River in 1869–70. See: [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba)

### Public Accounts Committee {#public-accounts-committee}
The House of Commons committee that reviews the Auditor General's reports, chaired by an opposition MP. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs), [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Public Accounts of Canada {#public-accounts-of-canada}
The government's financial statements, reporting what was actually spent and collected. The Auditor General audits them. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Public bill {#public-bill}
A bill that applies to everyone, whether a government bill or a private member's bill. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Public government {#public-government}
A government that serves everyone who lives in an area, whatever their background, as opposed to an Indigenous government that serves its own members. Nunavut's government is the main example. See: [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut), [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government)

### Public law {#public-law}
Law that works the same way across Canada, including Quebec: criminal, constitutional and administrative law. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Public Prosecution Service of Canada {#public-prosecution-service-of-canada}
The federal prosecutor for drug offences and other federal offences, and for all criminal prosecutions in the three territories. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### Public service {#public-service}
The permanent, non-partisan staff of government departments, hired on merit, who serve whichever party is in power. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Public Service Commission {#public-service-commission}
The body, reporting to Parliament, that protects the merit system in the federal public service and approves public servants' requests to run as candidates. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Puisne judges {#puisne-judges}
The eight Supreme Court judges other than the Chief Justice (pronounced "puny"). See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court)

## Q

### Quebec Act, 1774 {#quebec-act-1774}
The British law that restored French civil law for "property and civil rights" in the colony while keeping English criminal law, the root of Quebec's civil law today. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Quebec Pension Plan {#quebec-pension-plan}
Quebec's own pension plan, which it runs instead of the Canada Pension Plan. Section 94A makes this possible. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Quebec Veto Reference {#quebec-veto-reference}
The December 1982 Supreme Court opinion that Quebec had no veto over patriation by convention, so the Constitution Act, 1982 applies in Quebec like everywhere else. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Queen's Park {#queens-park}
Where Ontario's legislature meets in Toronto, often used as shorthand for the Ontario government. See: [Ontario](/provinces/ontario)

### Question Period {#question-period}
The up to 45 minutes each sitting day when MPs, mostly from the opposition, question the Prime Minister and ministers in the House. See: [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

## R

### R. {#r-crown}
Short for *Rex* or *Regina* (Latin for King or Queen), used in criminal case names such as *R. v. Smith* because the Crown prosecutes crimes. See: [The Crown](/learn/crown), [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### Ranked ballot {#ranked-ballot}
A voting method, also called the **alternative vote**, where voters rank candidates and the lowest are eliminated and their votes transferred until someone has a majority. Many parties use it to choose leaders. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post), [How parties choose leaders](/learn/elections/party-leaders)

### RCMP {#rcmp}
The Royal Canadian Mounted Police, the federal police force. Most provinces other than Ontario and Quebec, and all three territories, pay it to act as their provincial police. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### Reading in {#reading-in}
A court remedy where, instead of striking down a law, the court treats it as if it included missing words, as in *Vriend v. Alberta*. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

### Readings {#readings}
The stages of debate and voting a bill goes through in each chamber: **first reading** (introduced), **second reading** (debate on the principle), **committee stage**, **report stage** and **third reading** (final vote). See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Reasonable limits {#reasonable-limits}
Section 1 of the Charter: rights are subject only to limits that are reasonable, prescribed by law and demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society. The government must justify a limit using the Oakes test. See: [Reasonable limits: section 1](/learn/charter/reasonable-limits), [Rights: the Charter](/learn/charter)

### Recall {#recall}
A process letting voters petition to remove their member of the legislature between elections. Alberta and British Columbia are the only provinces that have it. See: [Alberta](/provinces/alberta), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Recognized party {#recognized-party}
A party with at least 12 MPs, which gets extra funding and speaking rights in the House. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Red River Resistance {#red-river-resistance}
The 1869–70 movement in which the Métis at Red River, led by Louis Riel, formed a provisional government and negotiated Manitoba's entry into Canada. See: [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba)

### Reference {#reference}
A government asking a court for its opinion on a legal question rather than deciding a real dispute. The federal cabinet can go directly to the Supreme Court; provinces go to their court of appeal first. Opinions are advisory but treated as binding. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Reference re Senate Reform {#reference-re-senate-reform}
The 2014 Supreme Court opinion that Senate elections or term limits need the 7/50 procedure and that abolishing the Senate needs unanimity. See: [The Senate](/learn/parliament/senate), [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula)

### Reference re Supreme Court Act, ss. 5 and 6 {#reference-re-supreme-court-act}
The 2014 Supreme Court opinion that the Quebec seats are part of the Court's composition, which can only be changed with unanimity. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula)

### Referendum {#referendum}
A public vote on a question. No referendum is legally required to amend the Constitution, but some provinces require one before their legislature votes on certain amendments. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula), [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Reform Act {#reform-act}
See [Parliament of Canada Act](/glossary#parliament-of-canada-act).

### Regional county municipality {#regional-county-municipality}
In Quebec, a body (*MRC*) that groups neighbouring municipalities for land-use planning and shared services. See: [Quebec](/provinces/quebec), [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities)

### Regional district {#regional-district}
In British Columbia, a federation of local governments covering the whole province, including rural land outside any city or town, that provides shared services and acts as the local government for rural areas. See: [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Regional veto {#regional-veto}
A 1996 federal law (not a constitutional amendment) under which federal ministers won't propose most amendments unless Ontario, Quebec, British Columbia, and enough Atlantic and Prairie provinces agree first. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula), [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982)

### Registered Indian {#registered-indian}
See [Status](/glossary#status).

### Regular Members {#regular-members}
In NWT and Nunavut, the MLAs outside cabinet, who act as an unofficial opposition. See: [Northwest Territories](/provinces/northwest-territories), [Nunavut](/provinces/nunavut)

### Regulations {#regulations}
Detailed rules made under a statute, usually by cabinet or a minister, which must stay within what the statute allows. See: [The legal system](/learn/law), [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Reservation {#reservation}
A power in the 1867 Act letting a Lieutenant Governor "reserve" a provincial bill for Ottawa's decision. Last used in Saskatchewan in 1961 and widely considered obsolete. See: [The Constitution Act, 1867](/learn/constitution/constitution-act-1867), [Reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers)

### Reserve {#reserve}
Land set apart for the use and benefit of a band under the Indian Act. Legal title stays with the Crown, so reserve land generally can't be mortgaged or sold like other land. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Reserve powers {#reserve-powers}
The few decisions the Governor General or a Lieutenant Governor can make on their own judgment instead of on advice, to protect responsible government, such as appointing a Prime Minister when no one clearly has confidence. They come from convention and are rarely used. See: [Reserve powers](/learn/crown/reserve-powers), [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### Residential schools {#residential-schools}
Boarding schools for Indigenous children, funded by the federal government and mostly run by churches, from the 1800s to the 1990s. More than 150,000 First Nations, Inuit and Métis children attended, many by force. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Residual power {#residual-power}
The power over anything not listed elsewhere. In Canada it belongs to Parliament under peace, order and good government. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

### Resolution {#resolution}
A formal vote in the Senate, the House of Commons or a provincial legislature, the building block of most constitutional amendments. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula)

### Responsible government {#responsible-government}
The core convention that the government must have the confidence of the elected assembly and must resign or seek an election if it loses it. Nova Scotia was the first colony to win it, in 1848. See: [Conventions: the unwritten rules](/learn/constitution/conventions), [Nova Scotia](/provinces/nova-scotia), [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence)

### Returning officer {#returning-officer}
The official who runs a federal election in each riding and checks the results. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day)

### Riding {#riding}
An electoral district that elects one member. Provinces also use names such as electoral division or **constituency**. See: [Elections and the Prime Minister](/learn/elections), [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day)

### Royal assent {#royal-assent}
The final step before a bill becomes law, given by the Governor General (or a Lieutenant Governor provincially) in the King's name. By convention it's always given to bills both chambers have passed. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law), [The Crown](/learn/crown), [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general)

### Royal Canadian Mint {#royal-canadian-mint}
The Crown corporation that manufactures Canada's coins, plus collector coins and bullion. It doesn't set interest rates or control inflation. See: [The Bank of Canada vs. the Mint](/learn/government/bank-of-canada-vs-mint), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Royal Proclamation of 1763 {#royal-proclamation-of-1763}
King George III's declaration that Indigenous lands could be acquired only by the Crown, with the agreement of the nation concerned. It's the root of the treaty process and is still mentioned in s. 25 of the Charter. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Royal recommendation {#royal-recommendation}
The requirement (s. 54) that spending of public money first be recommended by the Governor General, which in practice means only the government can propose new spending. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

## S

### Secession Reference {#secession-reference}
*Reference re Secession of Quebec* (1998): no province can secede unilaterally, but a clear majority on a clear question would create a duty to negotiate. It named four unwritten constitutional principles. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases), [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Secretary of state {#secretary-of-state}
A junior minister, also called a minister of state, who helps with a specific area. Whether they're full cabinet members has varied. See: [Cabinet, the PMO and the Privy Council Office](/learn/elections/cabinet-and-pmo)

### Section 1 {#section-1}
See [Reasonable limits](/glossary#reasonable-limits).

### Section 33 {#section-33}
See [Notwithstanding clause](/glossary#notwithstanding-clause).

### Section 35 {#section-35}
The section of the Constitution Act, 1982, outside the Charter, that recognizes and affirms the existing Aboriginal and treaty rights of First Nations, Inuit and Métis. Courts have defined those rights case by case. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Section 52 {#section-52}
The supremacy clause of the Constitution Act, 1982: the Constitution is the supreme law, and conflicting laws are of no force or effect. See: [The Constitution](/learn/constitution), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Section 92A {#section-92a}
A section added to the 1867 Act in 1982 confirming provincial power over non-renewable natural resources, forestry and electricity, including taxing them and regulating their export to other provinces. See: [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers), [Alberta](/provinces/alberta)

### Section 96 courts {#section-96-courts}
See [Superior court](/glossary#superior-court).

### Security of tenure {#security-of-tenure}
One of the three supports of judicial independence: judges hold office until retirement and can't be removed for unpopular decisions. See: [Judges: appointment and independence](/learn/law/judges), [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Self-government {#self-government}
Indigenous nations making their own laws and running their own affairs instead of being governed under the Indian Act, usually through negotiated agreements that vary from nation to nation. See: [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government), [Yukon](/provinces/yukon)

### Senate {#senate}
Parliament's appointed upper house, with 105 senators whose seats are divided by region. It reviews and amends bills and can legally defeat them, but by convention usually defers to the elected House. See: [The Senate](/learn/parliament/senate), [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Senate Ethics Officer {#senate-ethics-officer}
The official who administers senators' own ethics code, separate from the Conflict of Interest and Ethics Commissioner. See: [Officers of Parliament](/learn/government/watchdogs)

### Senatorial clause {#senatorial-clause}
Section 51A of the 1867 Act: no province can have fewer MPs than senators. It guarantees Prince Edward Island at least four MPs. See: [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island)

### Session {#session}
A division of a Parliament. It opens with a Speech from the Throne and ends with prorogation or dissolution. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament)

### Single transferable vote {#single-transferable-vote}
An electoral system (STV) with larger ridings that each elect several MPs, where voters rank candidates. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### Single-member plurality {#single-member-plurality}
See [First-past-the-post](/glossary#first-past-the-post).

### Single-tier and two-tier municipalities {#single-tier-and-two-tier-municipalities}
A **single-tier** municipality handles all local services; in a **two-tier** system, an upper-tier regional or county government handles some services and lower-tier municipalities inside it handle others. See: [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities), [Ontario](/provinces/ontario)

### Sovereignty-association {#sovereignty-association}
The proposal in Quebec's 1980 referendum, which asked for a mandate to negotiate political sovereignty with an economic association with Canada. The "No" side won. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [Quebec](/provinces/quebec)

### Sparrow {#sparrow}
*Sparrow* (1990), the first s. 35 test: a government can limit an Aboriginal right only with a valid objective, such as conservation, and in a way that respects the honour of the Crown, including giving the right priority. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35)

### Speaker {#speaker}
An MP elected by the other members to run debates neutrally. The Speaker votes only to break a tie. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Special ballot {#special-ballot}
A way to vote at any Elections Canada office or by mail, including from abroad. Once registered for it, you can't switch to voting in person. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day)

### Special Committee on Electoral Reform {#special-committee-on-electoral-reform}
A House of Commons committee created in 2016 that studied alternatives to first-past-the-post and recommended a referendum. The government decided in 2017 not to change the system. See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### Specific claim {#specific-claim}
A claim that the Crown broke a promise in an existing treaty or mismanaged First Nations land or money under the Indian Act. It's settled by negotiation, the courts or the Specific Claims Tribunal. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties)

### Speech from the Throne {#speech-from-the-throne}
The speech, written by the government and read by the Governor General, that opens each session and sets out the government's plans. The vote on it is a confidence vote. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [The Crown](/learn/crown)

### Standing committee {#standing-committee}
A permanent House or Senate committee covering a subject such as finance or health. Most bills go to one. See: [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### Stare decisis {#stare-decisis}
See [Precedent](/glossary#precedent).

### Status {#status}
The legal standing of a person registered under the Indian Act, also called being a **registered Indian**. It carries certain rights and benefits, and is separate from identity and from band membership. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Statute {#statute}
A law passed by Parliament or a provincial or territorial legislature, also called an Act. See: [The legal system](/learn/law)

### Statute of Westminster, 1931 {#statute-of-westminster-1931}
The British law that ended Britain's power to legislate for Canada without its consent, but at Canada's request left the BNA Acts out, so the British Parliament remained the formal amender until 1982. See: [1982: Bringing the Constitution home](/learn/constitution/patriation-1982), [The Constitution](/learn/constitution)

### Statutory spending {#statutory-spending}
Spending already authorized by an existing law, such as Old Age Security or interest on the public debt, so it doesn't need an annual vote. **Voted spending** needs an annual appropriation act. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Strategic voting {#strategic-voting}
Choosing a candidate you think can win rather than your first choice, to avoid "splitting the vote". See: [First-past-the-post and the alternatives](/learn/elections/first-past-the-post)

### Strong mayor powers {#strong-mayor-powers}
Extra powers Ontario has given the mayors of some municipalities since 2022, such as proposing the budget and hiring senior staff. See: [Ontario](/provinces/ontario), [Municipalities](/learn/federalism/municipalities)

### Summary conviction offence {#summary-conviction-offence}
A less serious criminal offence, with simpler procedure and lower maximum penalties, such as causing a disturbance. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law)

### Superior court {#superior-court}
The main trial court of general jurisdiction in each province and territory, also called a **section 96 court** because its judges are appointed and paid by the federal government. It hears the most serious criminal cases, major civil suits and divorce. Its name varies: Court of King's Bench, Superior Court or, confusingly, Supreme Court. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts), [The legal system](/learn/law), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Supplementary Estimates {#supplementary-estimates}
Requests during the year, labelled (A), (B) and (C), for money not in the Main Estimates. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Supply {#supply}
Parliament's grant of money to the Crown. Votes on supply are confidence votes. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [Majority, minority and confidence](/learn/parliament/confidence)

### Supply bill {#supply-bill}
A bill, also called an **appropriation bill**, that approves the amounts in the estimates. Once it passes and receives royal assent, the government can spend that money. See: [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget), [Government in action](/learn/government)

### Supreme Court of Canada {#supreme-court-of-canada}
Canada's final court of appeal since 1949, with nine judges, at least three from Quebec. It hears most cases only with leave, answers references, and its decisions bind every other court. See: [The Supreme Court of Canada](/learn/law/supreme-court), [The legal system](/learn/law)

## T

### Tax Court of Canada {#tax-court-of-canada}
The federal court that hears disputes between taxpayers and the federal government about federal taxes. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Tax points {#tax-points}
A form of transfer where Ottawa lowers its own tax rates so provinces can raise theirs by the same amount. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization)

### Terms of Union {#terms-of-union}
The conditions on which a colony joined Canada, now part of the Constitution. Examples include British Columbia's railway promise, PEI's ferry promise and Newfoundland's 1949 terms. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador), [Prince Edward Island](/provinces/prince-edward-island), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Territorial Formula Financing {#territorial-formula-financing}
An annual unconditional federal grant (TFF) to each territorial government, the territories' version of equalization and their largest source of funding. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization), [Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories)

### Territory {#territory}
Yukon, the Northwest Territories or Nunavut. A territory has an elected legislature and premier like a province, but its powers are delegated by a federal Act rather than guaranteed by the Constitution. See: [Why territories aren't provinces](/learn/federalism/territories), [Provinces and territories](/provinces)

### TFF {#tff}
See [Territorial Formula Financing](/glossary#territorial-formula-financing).

### Time allocation and closure {#time-allocation-and-closure}
House procedures the government can use to limit how long debate on a bill lasts at a given stage. See: [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)

### Tort {#tort}
In the common law, a wrong such as harm caused by carelessness, governed mostly by court decisions. Quebec's equivalent is civil liability. See: [Common law and civil law](/learn/law/common-law-and-civil-law)

### Transfers {#transfers}
Federal payments to provinces and territories that move money from where it's raised to where it's spent. The four major ones are the Canada Health Transfer, Canada Social Transfer, equalization and Territorial Formula Financing. See: [Transfers and equalization](/learn/federalism/money-and-equalization), [Federalism: who does what](/learn/federalism)

### Treasury Board {#treasury-board}
A cabinet committee, set up by law and supported by its Secretariat (TBS), that acts as the government's management board: spending controls, the estimates and public service employment rules. See: [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments), [How federal spending gets approved](/learn/government/the-budget)

### Treaty {#treaty}
A formal agreement between an Indigenous nation or nations and the Crown, setting out rights and obligations on both sides. Canada recognizes 70 historic treaties (1701–1923) plus modern treaties since 1975. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Treaty of Niagara {#treaty-of-niagara}
The 1764 meeting at Niagara, recorded in wampum belts, which many Indigenous people and scholars see as accepting the Royal Proclamation's promises as a nation-to-nation relationship. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties)

### Treaty rights {#treaty-rights}
Rights that come from agreements with the Crown, including modern land claims agreements. Section 35 protects them. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties)

### Truth and Reconciliation Commission {#truth-and-reconciliation-commission}
The commission (TRC), created in 2008, that documented the history of residential schools and in 2015 issued 94 Calls to Action. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### Tsilhqot'in {#tsilhqotin}
*Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia* (2014), the first time a court declared Aboriginal title to a specific area of land. See: [Section 35: Aboriginal and treaty rights](/learn/indigenous/section-35), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Two-year rule {#two-year-rule}
The rule that a total prison sentence of two years or more is served in a federal penitentiary, and shorter sentences in a provincial jail. See: [Criminal law: who does what](/learn/law/criminal-law), [The division of powers](/learn/federalism/division-of-powers)

## U

### Umbrella Final Agreement {#umbrella-final-agreement}
The 1993 framework agreement signed by Canada, Yukon and the Council for Yukon Indian People for individual Yukon First Nations land claim agreements. See: [Yukon](/provinces/yukon)

### Unanimity procedure {#unanimity-procedure}
The strictest amending procedure (s. 41): the Senate, the House of Commons and all ten provincial legislatures. It covers the monarchy, the Supreme Court's composition and the amending formula itself, and has never been successfully used. See: [Why changing the Constitution is so hard](/learn/constitution/amending-formula), [Could Canada drop the monarchy?](/learn/crown/republic-question)

### Unceded {#unceded}
Land that Indigenous nations never gave up by treaty. This includes most of British Columbia and, because the Peace and Friendship Treaties didn't surrender land, territories in the Maritimes. See: [Treaties](/learn/indigenous/treaties), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia), [Indigenous peoples and the Crown](/learn/indigenous)

### UNDRIP {#undrip}
The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted in 2007. Federal (2021) and BC (2019) laws commit those governments to bring their laws into line with it. See: [Self-government](/learn/indigenous/self-government), [British Columbia](/provinces/british-columbia)

### Unicity {#unicity}
The name often used for the 1972 merger of Winnipeg and its surrounding municipalities into a single city. See: [Manitoba](/provinces/manitoba)

### Unified family court {#unified-family-court}
A court in some provinces where superior court judges handle all family matters in one place. See: [How the courts are organized](/learn/law/courts)

### Unincorporated {#unincorporated}
Describes an area that isn't part of any municipality, where services come from the province or territory or from local bodies. See: [Newfoundland and Labrador](/provinces/newfoundland-and-labrador), [Yukon](/provinces/yukon)

### Unwritten principles {#unwritten-principles}
Deeper principles courts have recognized underlying the Constitution, such as federalism, democracy, constitutionalism and the rule of law, and respect for minorities. They help courts interpret the written text. See: [The Constitution](/learn/constitution), [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

## V

### VIA Rail Canada {#via-rail-canada}
The federal Crown corporation that runs intercity passenger trains. See: [Crown corporations](/learn/government/crown-corporations)

### Voted spending {#voted-spending}
See [Statutory spending](/glossary#statutory-spending).

### Vriend v. Alberta {#vriend-v-alberta}
*Vriend v. Alberta* (1998), where the Supreme Court held that leaving sexual orientation out of Alberta's human rights law violated equality rights, and read it in. See: [Landmark Charter cases](/learn/charter/landmark-cases)

## W

### Westminster system {#westminster-system}
The British-style parliamentary system Canada uses, in which the government sits in the legislature and needs its confidence, and a permanent non-partisan public service serves whichever party governs. See: [The big picture](/learn/big-picture), [Departments and the public service](/learn/government/departments)

### Whip {#whip}
An MP whose job is to organize the party's members, assign them to committees and make sure they're present and voting with the party. See: [Parliament](/learn/parliament), [Question Period and committees](/learn/parliament/question-period-and-committees)

### White Paper (1969) {#white-paper-1969}
A federal proposal to abolish the Indian Act and status entirely. First Nations rejected it strongly as assimilation by another name, and it was withdrawn. See: [The Indian Act](/learn/indigenous/indian-act)

### Writ {#writ}
The formal order to hold an election in a riding. Election day must fall between the 36th and 50th day after the writs are issued. See: [How a federal election runs](/learn/elections/election-day)

### Written declaration {#written-declaration}
A way of giving royal assent without a ceremony, allowed since the 2002 *Royal Assent Act*. The traditional ceremony must still happen at least twice a year. See: [The Governor General and Lieutenant Governors](/learn/crown/governor-general), [How a bill becomes law](/learn/parliament/how-a-bill-becomes-law)
